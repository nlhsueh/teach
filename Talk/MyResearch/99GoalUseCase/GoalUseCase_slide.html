Bibliographic Metadata & Historical Significance: - Paper Title: Analyzing User Requirements by Use Cases: A Goal-Driven Approach - Authors: Jonathan Lee (Professor) and Nien-Lin Xue (PhD Student), Department of Computer Science and Information Engineering, National Central University, Taiwan. - Venue: IEEE Software, Vol. 16, No. 4 (July/August 1999), pp. 92–101 (Feature Article). - Role in Research Lineage: This seminal IEEE Software paper introduced the Goal-Driven Use Cases (GDUC) methodology, establishing the faceted goal classification scheme (Competence, View, Content), systematic derivation of original and extension use cases, and the formal three-step goal interaction evaluation pipeline that directly served as the foundation for the authors' subsequent 2001 IST paper on structuring Fuzzy Object-Oriented Models.

Article Structure Overview: 1. Introduction (pp. 92–93): Contrasts user-centered use-case strengths with two major limitations (NFRs and requirement interactions), introducing GDUC's four pillars. 2. Goal-Driven Use Cases & Actors (pp. 93–94): Synthesizes Jacobson, Dardenne, and Mylopoulos; defines primary and secondary actors. 3. Identifying Goals (pp. 94–95): Introduces the 3-facet classification scheme (Competence, View, Content). 4. Building Use-Case Models (pp. 95–97, p. 100): Details Original Use Cases, Extension Use Cases (3 triggers), Refinement via `uses`, and the boxed Sample Use Case (`Plan a meeting`). 5. Goal Evaluation (pp. 97–99): Presents the 3-step evaluation pipeline, 5 predicates, direct vs. side effects, and formal system-level interaction equations for original–original, original–extension, and extension–extension goal pairs. 6. Conclusion & Future Work (pp. 99–100): Summarizes GDUC benefits and previews Fuzzy OOM and AHP prioritization.

Problem Statement & Procedural Logic Inspiration (pp. 92–93): While Jacobson's use cases excel at user-centered complexity management, Dano et al. and Regnell et al. noted their weakness in formalization and large-scale structuring. Lee and Xue identify two deeper requirements-engineering gaps: 1. Nonfunctional requirements (NFRs) such as convenience, performance, and flexibility have no principled place in classical use-case diagrams. 2. Interactions among requirements (conflicts and synergies) remain invisible until late design. Drawing on Georgeff & Lansky's procedural logic ("every process serves a purpose"), GDUC binds each use case to an explicit goal to be achieved, optimized, or maintained.

Synthesis of Prior Work (pp. 93–94): GDUC fuses two complementary traditions: - Jacobson's Use-Case Driven Engineering: provides `extends` (behavioral extension) and `uses` (factoring common behavior) alongside basic/alternative courses. - Goal-Oriented RE (Dardenne et al. 1993; Mylopoulos et al. 1992): explains why requirements exist and recognizes that NFRs are satisfied to degrees rather than all-or-nothing. GDUC operationalizes this fusion through a clean three-step acquisition workflow (Actors -> Faceted Goals -> Use-Case Models), followed by a three-step evaluation workflow.

Actors & Three-Facet Taxonomy (pp. 93–95): 1. Competence Facet: Rigid (R) goals define minimum requirements that must be completely satisfied; Soft (S) goals define desirable properties satisfied partially and are weakly dependent on their rigid goal (e.g., `MaxConvenientSchedule` and `MaxNumberofParticipants` are meaningless if `MeetingRequestSatisfied` fails). 2. View Facet: Actor-specific (A) goals capture an individual actor's objectives, whereas System-specific (Y) goals capture properties the system must uphold across all users (e.g., dynamically replanning to accommodate a more important meeting -> `SupportFlexibility`). 3. Content Facet: Functional (F) goals are achieved by executing a sequence of operations, whereas Nonfunctional (N) goals act as constraints qualifying their associated functional goals.

Worked Classification Analysis (pp. 94–95): Notice how every goal receives an unambiguous triple (Competence, View, Content): - Only (R, A, F) goals become Original Goals — guaranteeing that the base use-case model covers every actor's mandatory functional baseline without clutter. - Any goal deviating on even one facet (Soft, System-specific, or Nonfunctional) becomes an Extension Goal, handled modularly via `extends` use cases. - Notice also the distinction between Optimize vs. Maintain: `Make a convenient schedule` optimizes convenience beyond the basic meeting plan, whereas `Increase attendance` maintains the full participant count when recovery courses threaten to drop attendees.

Critical Distinction Between `extends` and `uses` in GDUC (pp. 95–97): Although both `extends` and `uses` insert an additional subsequence into a base use-case flow, GDUC draws a sharp semantic boundary between them: - In an `extends` relationship, BOTH the original use case and the extension use case own explicit goals to achieve, optimize, or maintain. - In a `uses` relationship, the extracted abstract use case exists purely to enhance modularity and reusability and has NO goal associated with it. For instance, both `Increase attendance` and `Make a convenient schedule` may need to extend a meeting's date range; factoring `Extend date range` via `uses` eliminates duplication without inventing artificial goals.

Structural Walkthrough of Figure 2 (pp. 95–96): - Two original use cases satisfy the two rigid, actor-specific, functional goals: `Plan a meeting` (Initiator -> `MeetingRequestSatisfied`) and `Register a meeting` (Participant -> `RegisterAMeeting`). - Four extension use cases extend `Plan a meeting`: 1. `Increase attendance` -> Maintain `MaxNumberofParticipants (S, A, N)`. 2. `Make a convenient schedule` -> Optimize `MaxConvenientSchedule (S, A, N)`. 3. `Keep appropriate performance` -> Optimize `AppropriatePerformance (S, A, N)`. 4. `Accommodate important meetings` -> Optimize `SupportFlexibility (S, Y, F)`. - One abstract use case (`Extend date range`) is shared via `uses` by both `Increase attendance` and `Make a convenient schedule`.

Requirements Elicitation Value of Recovery vs. Failure Courses (pp. 95, 100): Separating alternative courses into Recovery Courses (restoring the original goal `MeetingRequestSatisfied`) and Failure Courses (handling unrecoverable failure) directly uncovers missing requirements! As the authors note on p. 95, the original Meeting Scheduler problem statement never specified when a meeting should be canceled; forcing analysts to define the failure course for `MeetingRequestSatisfied` immediately exposed this elicitation gap. Furthermore, notice how recovery actions (e.g., proposing a low-importance participant withdraw) can impair the soft goal `MaxNumberofParticipants`, motivating the `Increase attendance` extension use case to maintain that soft goal.

Evaluation Pipeline Architecture (pp. 96–97): Figure 3 visually summarizes GDUC's bottom-up evaluation mechanics: - Matrix (a) has rows U1..Um and columns G1..Gn, populated with the 5 predicates (`Satisfied`, `Denied`, `Satisfiable`, `Deniable`, `Independent`). - Matrix (b) transforms pairs of columns from (a) into pairwise goal relations (`Conflicting`, `Cooperative`, `Irrelevant`) per use case Uk. - Matrix (c) amalgamates rows across the use-case model into a single system-level verdict (`Conflicting`, `Cooperative`, `Counterbalanced`, `Irrelevant`) for every goal pair (Gi, Gj).

Side-Effect Mechanics in Figure 4 (p. 97): A major contribution of GDUC is making hidden side effects explicit: - Arrow (c) captures positive spillover from achieving an original goal to its weakly dependent soft extension goals. - Arrow (d) highlights a crucial rule: an extension use case generally does NOT impair its own original goal, EXCEPT when the extension serves a system-specific goal (like `SupportFlexibility` overriding an individual initiator's meeting). - Arrow (e) exposes cross-extension interference — both positive (`Increase attendance` extending the date range also makes `MaxConvenientSchedule` satisfiable) and negative (`Keep appropriate performance` terminating negotiation makes `MaxConvenientSchedule` deniable).

Use-Case vs. System Level Semantics (pp. 98–99): - At the use-case level, `make a convenient schedule` yields `satisfiable` for `MaxConvenientSchedule` and `deniable` for `AppropriatePerformance`, giving `cf_{make a convenient schedule}(MaxConvenientSchedule, AppropriatePerformance) = True`. - At the system level, two goals can cooperate in one use case while conflicting in another; the pair ⟨cp, cf⟩ = ⟨True, True⟩ captures this nuanced "Counterbalanced" state instead of collapsing it prematurely. - Distinction from QARCC (Boehm & In 1996): QARCC targets generic, domain-independent conflicts between architectural strategies and quality attributes (e.g., layered architecture improving portability at the expense of performance), whereas GDUC discovers requirement-level conflicts grounded in the target domain's concrete use cases.

Recursive System-Level Derivation Equations (pp. 98–99, Figure 5): GDUC's derivation rules are cleanly stratified by weak dependence: 1. Original–Original (Gi, Gj): Checks cooperation/conflict over their respective original use cases Ui and Uj. 2. Original–Extension (Gi, Gj1): Because Gj1 is only achieved/optimized if its base original goal Gj is satisfied, the system-level relation Rs(Gi, Gj) must be included alongside use-case evaluations over Ui and Uj1. In Proof 1, since G_MCS extends G_MRS and cp(G_MRS, G_MRS) = True (reflexivity), G_MRS and G_MCS cooperate. 3. Extension–Extension (Gi1, Gj1): Combines cross-level interactions (Gi1 with Gj, and Gi with Gj1) plus direct extension use-case evaluations (Ui1 and Uj1). In Proof 2, evaluating `make a convenient schedule` (U_MCS) and `keep appropriate performance` (U_KAP) yields `cf(G_MCS, G_AP) = True`, rigorously proving the system-level conflict between convenience and performance.

Conclusion & Research Continuity (pp. 99–100): GDUC demonstrated that goals play a dual role in requirements acquisition: structuring use-case specifications and formally evaluating requirement interactions. Notably, the paper's two concluding future-work commitments — (1) extending object-oriented analysis models with fuzzy logic to utilize GDUC's goal interactions, and (2) prioritizing goals via AHP — became the exact core contributions of Lee, Xue & Kuo (IST 2001, "Structuring requirement specifications with goals"), forming a cohesive multi-year research arc.
