Bibliographic & Research Overview: This journal article by Jonathan Lee, Shang-Pin Ma, Lien Fu Lai, Nien Lin Hsueh, and Yong-Yi Fanjiang, published in the International Journal of Intelligent Systems (Vol. 20, pp. 1137 to 1160, 2005; DOI 10.1002/int.20115), introduces an automated software engineering methodology—Task-Based Conceptual Graphs (TBCG)—to solve university course timetabling. Core Thesis: Rather than hard-coding scheduling algorithms whose logic becomes brittle whenever institutional rules change, TBCG models timetabling specifications graphically and formally across four views (Domain, State, Functional, and Behavioral models). After verifying consistency, a six-level Blackboard system automatically translates TBCG specifications into an executable CLIPS expert system and Oracle object-relational database schemas, allowing maintenance and constraint changes to be performed directly at the specification level.

Structural Map of the Paper: Section 1 (Introduction, pp. 1137 to 1139) defines timetabling and identifies four enduring engineering challenges. Section 2 (Related Work, pp. 1139 to 1141) surveys 30 years of timetabling research across Operations Research (sequential, cluster, meta-heuristic), Human-Machine Interaction, and Artificial Intelligence (constraint programming, expert systems, neural networks). Section 3 (Problem Statements, pp. 1141 to 1142) specifies the university course timetabling problem at National Central University (NCU) CSIE with 3 actors and 7 constraints. Section 4 (Conceptual Modeling through TBCG, pp. 1143 to 1155) formalizes TBSM in Conceptual Graphs (4.1) and details operationalization into CLIPS via a Blackboard architecture (4.2). Section 5 (NCU Timetabling System, pp. 1155 to 1158) demonstrates generated Oracle schemas (5.1) and CLIPS knowledge base rules (5.2). Section 6 (Conclusion, p. 1158) summarizes architectural advantages.

Engineering Motivation (Section 1): Following Gunadhi et al. (1996), the paper explicitly scopes the university course timetabling problem with three practical assumptions: (1) students are already assigned or select classes themselves; (2) constraints partition cleanly into hard (mandatory) vs. soft (desirable); and (3) the practical objective is finding a feasible timetable satisfying all hard constraints and maximizing soft constraint satisfaction, rather than optimizing a single rigid mathematical objective function. Why Automated Software Engineering Matters: In conventional timetabling implementations, adding or relaxing a single departmental rule requires modifying tangled imperative code. By elevating the specification into first-order-logic-backed Task-Based Conceptual Graphs (TBCG) that automatically compile into CLIPS rules and database schemas, policy maintenance becomes transparent, explainable, and rapid.

Comparative Literature Synthesis (Section 2): The paper organizes three decades of timetabling literature into three major families: 1. Operations Research (Section 2.1): Sequential graph-coloring methods (de Werra, 1985, 1996, 1997) struggle with scale and dynamic customization; cluster methods (Tripathy, 1984, 1992; Ferland & Roy, 1985) risk poor timetable quality due to early fixed clustering (Burke & Petrovic, 2002); meta-heuristics (Hertz, 1991, 1992; Schaerf, 1999; Moscato & Norman, 1992) achieve strong quality at steep computational cost. 2. Human-Machine Interaction (Section 2.2): Systems like Mulvey (1982) and Johnson (1993) aid clerical bookkeeping and interactive refinement but remain laborious at scale. 3. Artificial Intelligence (Section 2.3): Constraint logic programming (Boizumault et al., 1996; Deris et al., 2000; Valouxis & Housos, 2003) and expert systems (Gunadhi et al., 1996; Solotorevsky et al., 1994 RAPS; Dhar & Ranganathan, 1990 PROTEUS) separate knowledge from algorithms, whereas Hopfield neural networks (Smith et al., 1995) suffer heavy computational overhead (Mausser & Magazine, 1996). TBCG builds directly on AI's separation of concerns while automating the entire specification-to-code lifecycle.

Problem Statement & NCU Benchmark (Section 3): Following Schaerf's (1999) taxonomy (school, course, and examination timetabling), this study addresses university course timetabling using real operational data from the Department of Computer Science and Information Engineering at National Central University (NCU): 19 faculty members, over 550 students, 42 subjects (1 to 5 lectures/week based on credit hours), 40 weekly timeslots, and 6 classrooms. Constraint Partitioning: Constraints 1 through 5 are Hard Constraints (Level C0, strictly mandatory): no instructor double-booking, no overlap among required courses of the same class, single course per classroom per slot, classroom availability respected, and room capacity >= registered students. Constraints 6 and 7 are Soft Constraints (relaxable): matching instructor preference/exclusion sets, and avoiding 3+ consecutive lectures (e.g., splitting a 3-credit course into 2 consecutive slots + 1 isolated slot on another day).

Four-Stage TBCG Automation Pipeline (Section 4, Figure 1): Figure 1 highlights the paper's core software engineering loop: 1. Specification Acquisition: Task-Based Specification Methodology (TBSM; Lee, Lai, & Huang, 1996) organizes domain knowledge, functional requirements, and problem-solving methods around tasks, while Conceptual Graphs (CGs; Sowa, 1984) supply first-order-logic semantics. A Contextual Retrieval Mechanism (Lai, Lee, & Yang, 1999) uses fuzzy similarity and purpose-directed analogy to retrieve reusable TBCGs from the repository. 2. Verification (Lee & Lai, 1998): Checks both static model consistency (constraint networks and relaxation repair) and dynamic process consistency (inter-level and intra-level transitions). 3. Operationalization: Translates verified TBCGs into executable CLIPS code. 4. Specification-Level Maintenance: Crucially, the feedback arrow labeled "Maintenance" returns directly to TBCG specifications rather than to generated CLIPS code.

Formal Foundations of TBCG (Section 4.1): TBCG unifies TBSM's multi-level task refinement (task / method / subtask, inspired by Chandrasekaran, 1990) with Sowa's Conceptual Graphs extended by Delugach's (1991) dynamic demons and Eshner/Pfeiffer's constraint overlays. - Static vs. Dynamic Split: Domain Models map terms to ENTITY concepts, attributes via (attr), and types via (chrc). State Models overlay constraint actors onto state graphs and use demons whose inputs are STATE objects plus TASK tokens. - Functional Semantics: Preconditions, protections, and rigid postconditions are crisp propositions, whereas soft postconditions are modeled as fuzzy propositions (Lee & Kuo, 1998). - Behavioral Semantics: Task State Expressions (TSE) extend regular expressions and compile into state-transition diagrams using an initiator demon, a START state, conditional test tasks (b? and not-b?), and a (final) monadic relation.

Task Hierarchy & Conflict Resolution (Section 4.1, Figure 2): Figure 2 illustrates how TBCG tames scheduling complexity through modular task decomposition (task -> method -> subtasks). - At the top level, M-T0 controls the loop (b1(T1, T2))* over all instructors. - Inside Schedule-An-Instructor (T2), M-T2 specifies the TSE (b2(T21, T22, b3(T23)))*: while unscheduled courses remain (b2), select a course (T21), attempt to schedule it (T22), and invoke Conflict-Resolution (T23) only when a conflict occurs (b3 is true). - When invoked, M-T23 resolves conflicts by relaxing soft constraints through Schedule-With-Flexibility (T231), optionally reducing the instructor's exclusive set (T232), or enlarging the preference set (T233).

Detailed Anatomy of Schedule-A-Course (Section 4.1, Figures 5 and 6): Figure 5 demonstrates how static data structure and dynamic state-transition semantics coexist cleanly inside a single TBCG task specification: - Domain Model: Models Instructor, Course, and Available-Location along with single-valued and multi-valued attributes. - State Model: Specifies that the state "[current] <- (Instructor) -> (instruct) -> [Course] -> (current)" transitions to "... -> [Course] -> (scheduled)" if and only if (1) the constraint hierarchy T22-L1 through T22-L5 is satisfied (at least through hard level C0) and (2) the task token Schedule-A-Course is performed. - Figure 6 in the paper zooms into constraint T22-L1, separating its "rule" part (checking satisfaction) from its "fix-rule" part (repairing violations).

Blackboard Operationalization Architecture (Section 4.2, Figure 7): To bridge the gap between graphical conceptual graphs and a complete multi-component software system (database, expert system rules, and user interface), the authors employ a Blackboard problem-solving model organized into six abstraction levels: Task, Model (DM, SM, FM, BM), Database, Rule, UI Information, and Knowledge Base. Opportunistic & Incremental Synthesis: Knowledge Sources (KS) communicate exclusively by reading and writing changes on the blackboard. Whenever a new TBCG task specification or constraint edit is posted, the relevant KSs trigger incrementally to regenerate the affected deftemplates, defrules, UI metadata, and synthesized knowledge base—avoiding monolithic recompilation and enabling rapid iterative prototyping.

Systematic Code Generation Pipeline (Sections 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 5.1, and 5.2): This diagram summarizes the exact target artifacts produced by the TBCG CASE tool and Blackboard system: - From Domain Model (DM): One-to-one mapping of concept cardinalities (Table II) into CLIPS deftemplate constructs with single-field (slot) and multi-field (multislot) attributes plus a unique non-null id slot for entity integrity, then directly into Oracle object-relational tables (collection columns for multislots). - From State/Functional/Behavioral Models (SM, FM, BM): Modular CLIPS defmodules and defrules implementing constraint tests, violation repairs, high-salience protection invariants, and focus-stack control flow. - From UI Info Extraction: Initial Java front-end forms and interaction flows connected to the Oracle database via JDBC/ODBC.

Detailed Translation Mechanics (Sections 4.2.1, 4.2.2, 5.1, and 5.2): 1. Database & Integrity (4.2.1 & 5.1): For Schedule-A-Course, the Database Construction KS generates three CLIPS templates: Course, Instructor, and Available-Location. The Course template maps directly to the Oracle relation Course(id, state, name, session, class, required, credit-hours, {meeting}, {location}). Adding a mandatory unique id slot enforces key/entity integrity, while cascading cleanup on fact retraction enforces referential integrity. 2. Actor Operationalization (4.2.2): Table III enumerates 22 built-in actor primitives across test, manipulation, I/O, and arithmetic categories. New composite actors can be defined graphically as dataflow combinations of existing primitives and compiled automatically into CLIPS modules. 3. State Transitions & Hierarchy (4.2.2 & 5.2): Each constraint generates a test rule (e.g., T22-L1-rule asserting constraint-satisfaction) and a fix rule. Level-checking rules verify cumulative satisfaction from C0 downward, firing State-Transition only when satisfied. Protection rules carry the highest CLIPS salience so any invariant breach immediately rolls back the offending fact change.

System Deployment & Multi-Department Architecture (Section 5, Figure 11): Figure 11 shows the complete operational architecture deployed for National Central University. Each department (Department A, Department B) runs its own TBCG-generated executable expert system kernel paired with a Java front-end UI, while connecting through JDBC/ODBC over the campus network to a shared Oracle database. This design lets individual departments customize their local scheduling policies and constraints directly in their own TBCG specifications while maintaining campus-wide consistency on shared rooms and timeslots.

Critical Synthesis & Research Assessment (Section 6): The paper's enduring contribution lies in software engineering automation for knowledge-intensive scheduling systems: 1. Maintainability: Small logical changes at the code level often trigger unpredictable bugs, whereas editing a TBCG constraint overlay or task method is visual, formal, and verifiable prior to code regeneration. 2. Modularity: Mapping tasks to CLIPS defmodules and using a Blackboard architecture cleanly separates database schemas, constraint rules, invariants, and UI specifications. Methodological Limitations: As an architectural and modeling paper in IJIS, the evaluation focuses on expressiveness, formal translation correctness, and deployment feasibility on NCU CSIE's 42-course dataset rather than empirical runtime comparisons against meta-heuristic or integer programming benchmarks. Extending TBCG with quantitative soft-constraint scoring and large-scale multi-college benchmarks remains a natural avenue for follow-up work (e.g., the authors' subsequent IJAIT 2008 paper).
