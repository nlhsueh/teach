Bibliographic Metadata & Research Positioning: - Paper Title: Structuring requirement specifications with goals - Authors: Jonathan Lee (corresponding author), Nien-Lin Xue (National Central University), and Jong-Yih Kuo (Fu-Jen Catholic University). - Venue: Information and Software Technology (IST), Vol. 43 (2001), pp. 121–135, Elsevier Science B.V. - Research Lineage: This paper synthesizes and extends two foundational prior works by the same research group: Goal-Driven Use Cases (GDUC, Lee & Xue, IEEE Software 1999) and Fuzzy Object-Oriented Modeling (FOOM, Lee, Xue, Hsu & Yang, Information Sciences 1999). Whereas GDUC focused on eliciting and evaluating use cases with goals, this 2001 IST paper completes the pipeline all the way to structuring conflict-free Fuzzy Object-Oriented analysis models via a Goals Hierarchy, Stable Kernel, and systematic conflict-resolution strategies.

Structural Map of the Paper (Sections 1–6): - Section 1 (Introduction): Motivates the integration of goal-based RE, UML use cases, and fuzzy object-oriented modeling, illustrated through the Meeting Scheduler benchmark. - Section 2 (Related Work): Compares Dardenne et al., Mylopoulos et al., Anton (GBRAM), and Rolland et al., and classifies conflict-handling literature into Interaction, Design, and Structural conflicts alongside imprecise RE (RTA and Yen et al.). - Section 3 (Structuring Use Cases with Goals): Presents the 3-step GDUC process (actors, faceted goals, original & extension use cases). - Section 4 (Evaluating Goals Interactions): Formalizes 5 predicates between use cases and goals, use-case-level predicates cp/cf, and system-level interaction tuples Rs(Gi, Gj). - Section 5 (Structuring FOOM Specifications): Introduces the Goals Hierarchy backtracking algorithm, Stable Kernel selection via cooperative degree and Saaty's AHP, FOOM constructs, and conflict resolution (Prevention, Compromise, Competition). - Section 6 (Conclusion): Summarizes the four core benefits and outlines future directions (worth-oriented domains and university timetabling at NCU).

Theoretical Motivation (Section 1): Prior goal-oriented frameworks such as Dardenne et al. (KAOS), Mylopoulos et al. (NFR Framework), and Anton (GBRAM) demonstrated the value of goals for acquisition, justification, and conflict detection. However, they suffered from three structural limitations: 1. Lack of a de facto standard modeling language: Custom formalisms hinder industrial scalability compared to UML use cases and object models. 2. Disconnect between goal interaction analysis and specification structuring: Knowing that two NFRs conflict does not automatically tell analysts how to partition and incrementally build the object model without becoming overwhelmed by early conflict resolution. 3. Under-representation of imprecision: As Balzer et al. argued, informality and vagueness are inherent and even desirable in early specifications, yet crisp OO models force premature precision. Fig. 1 traces the proposed three-stage pipeline: - Stage 1: Use cases are extended with faceted goals (original & extension use cases) from domain descriptions and system requirements. - Stage 2: Interactions between NFR goals are evaluated at use-case and system levels (conflicting, cooperative, irrelevant, counterbalanced). - Stage 3: Interactions drive a Goals Hierarchy -> Stable Kernel (modeled in FOOM) -> incremental integration of conflicting alternatives via resolution strategies.

Comparative Positioning (Table 1 & Section 2.1): - Dardenne et al. (KAOS): Distinguishes nonoperational goals from operational constraints that restrict actions/objects to assign agent responsibilities. - Mylopoulos et al. (NFR Framework): Treats NFRs as softgoals rarely satisfied in a binary sense; design decisions contribute positively or negatively, evaluated via a labeling procedure. - Anton (GBRAM): Classifies goals into achievement goals (mapped to actions) and maintenance goals (mapped to NFRs), elaborated via obstacles and scenarios. - Rolland et al.: Iteratively pairs goal discovery with scenario authoring to populate a requirement chunks hierarchy. - Lee, Xue & Kuo's distinctive contribution: Unifies a three-facet goal taxonomy with standard UML use cases, derives four formal system-level interaction types, and directly uses those interactions to structure Fuzzy Object-Oriented Models (FOOM).

Literature Synthesis (Sections 2.2 & 2.3): The paper organizes conflict-handling research into three orthogonal categories: 1. Interaction conflicts: Arise from competing stakeholder objectives. Oz automates negotiation via compromise, compensation (adding conflict-free requirements), and dissolution (replacing contentious items). Easterbrook's Viewpoints tolerate inconsistency by branching self-consistent viewpoints in an inheritance hierarchy. van Lamsweerde et al. formalize weak conflicts (divergences) under boundary conditions using real-time temporal logic and divergence patterns. 2. Design conflicts: Arise relative to an architectural choice rather than requirement definitions. Boehm's QARCC operates inside WinWin using Theory W ("making everyone a winner") to flag quality-attribute risks. Vanwelkenhuysen's Design Requirement Embedding (DRE) records trade-off histories between conceptual design properties and requirements. 3. Structural conflicts: Same domain concept represented differently across views (entity vs. attribute); Spaccapietra resolves these via formal correspondences and generalization rules. For imprecise requirements (Section 2.3), Lee & Kuo's Requirements Trade-off Analysis (RTA) uses Zadeh's canonical form in test-score semantics and parameterized fuzzy and/or aggregation, while Yen et al. derive relative priority ratios from marginal willingness to sacrifice one requirement's satisfaction degree for another.

Goal-Driven Use Cases Mechanics (Section 3): - Original Goals & Use Cases: Every original goal is strictly ⟨Rigid, Actor-specific, Functional⟩. Mapping each original goal to an original use case guarantees the specification satisfies at least all actors' minimum functional requirements. In the Meeting Scheduler, `Plan a meeting` achieves `MeetingRequestSatisfied (R, A, F)`. Its basic course covers the nominal path; alternative courses split into recovery courses (recovering from strong schedule conflicts) and failure courses (canceling the meeting when unrecoverable). - Extension Goals & Use Cases: Extending Cockburn's goal-use case linkage, extension use cases are systematically created for three distinct reasons (Fig. 2 in the paper): * E1 (Soft goal): `Make a convenient schedule` optimizes `MaxConvenienceSchedule (S, A, N)`; `Increase attendance` maintains `MaxNumberOfParticipants (S, A, N)`. * E2 (System-specific goal): `Accommodate important meetings` achieves `SupportFlexibility (S, Y, F)` across all initiators rather than one actor's personal view. * E3 (Nonfunctional goal): `Keep appropriate performance` qualifies `Plan a meeting` with `AppropriatePerformance (S, A, N)`. - Weak Dependence: An extension goal's existence depends on its original goal, yet satisfying the original goal does not guarantee full satisfaction of extension goals (except partial satisfaction for soft goals).

Use-Case–Goal Predicates and Use-Case-Level Interactions (Sections 4.1 & 4.2): A use case is designed for its directly associated goal, yet executing it frequently alters non-associated goals through side effects (arrows c, d, e in Fig. 4). From these 5 predicates, pairwise goal interactions at the use-case level (with respect to a specific use case Uk) are derived via two boolean predicates: - cp_Uk(Gi, Gj) = True (Cooperative): both Gi and Gj increase in satisfaction (positively cooperative) or both decrease (negatively cooperative) after Uk executes. - cf_Uk(Gi, Gj) = True (Conflicting): one goal's satisfaction increases (satisfied/satisfiable) while the other's decreases (denied/deniable) after Uk executes. - Both False (Irrelevant): satisfaction degrees remain unchanged. Worked Example: For Uk = `make a convenient schedule`, its relationship with `MaxConvenienceSchedule` is `satisfiable` while its relationship with `AppropriatePerformance` is `deniable`. Hence cf_UMCS(MaxConvenienceSchedule, AppropriatePerformance) = True.

System-Level Aggregation Semantics (Section 4.3): Ebert (1998) noted that severe trade-offs occur predominantly among nonfunctional requirements. Therefore, Section 4.3 focuses system-level analysis on NFR goal pairs (Gi1, Gj1) associated with extension use cases Ui1 and Uj1, which extend original use cases Ui and Uj (Fig. 5 in the paper). Why include Ui and Uj in the disjunction? Because Gi1 is optimized or maintained only when its base original goal Gi is satisfied; thus any cooperative or conflicting effect induced by the base use cases Ui or Uj must propagate to the system-level relationship. Notice the crucial distinction of the fourth state, Counterbalanced ⟨True, True⟩: two goals can cooperate in one use case while conflicting in another! Both Conflicting and Counterbalanced pairs are prohibited from coexisting in the same conflict-free alternative in Section 5.1.

Goals Hierarchy Algorithm & Completeness (Section 5.1): Each root-to-leaf path in the Goals Hierarchy corresponds to one maximal conflict-free set of requirements (an alternative). In the paper's generic illustration (Fig. 6), functional goals G_FG form the root, and backtracking over NFRs G2–G6 yields three alternatives: {G_FG, G2, G3, G5}, {G_FG, G2, G6}, and {G_FG, G4, G3, G5}. Applied to the Meeting Scheduler (Fig. 7): - Root functional goals: G_MRS (MeetingRequestSatisfied) and G_PD (ParticipantDelegated). - Alternative A1 = {G_MRS, G_PD, G_MCS, G_MNOP, G_SF} (prioritizes convenience, attendance, and flexibility). - Alternative A2 = {G_MRS, G_PD, G_AP, G_MI} (prioritizes appropriate performance and minimal interaction). Because every alternative is guaranteed conflict-free, analysts can immediately construct a clean baseline model without premature trade-off paralysis.

Quantitative Kernel Selection (Section 5.2): Choosing which maximal conflict-free alternative becomes the Stable Kernel is governed by a two-tier evaluation: 1. Cooperative Degree: Rewards alternatives whose NFRs mutually reinforce one another (higher synergy, fewer internal tensions). 2. Importance Degree: When alternatives have equal cooperative degrees (as A1 and A2 do in the Meeting Scheduler), stakeholder priorities break the tie rigorously using Thomas Saaty's Analytic Hierarchy Process (AHP, 1982). Analysts elicit pairwise relative weights between goals to build a reciprocal matrix, then extract the normalized eigenvector via the eigenvalue method to obtain absolute weights. Summing the weights of goals in each alternative identifies the highest-value conflict-free kernel.

FOOM Formal Constructs (Sections 5.2.1–5.2.3): - Distinction between Imprecision and Uncertainty (Dubois & Prade 1988): Imprecision is the absence of a sharp boundary on an attribute value (modeled via linguistic degrees of participation, e.g., `strong`), whereas uncertainty reflects reliance/confidence in that information (modeled via fuzzy truth values `t` such as `true`, `fairly true`, `very true`). - Uncertain Fuzzy Association Quadruple: `(link attribute, ⟨x, y⟩, degree of participation, t)`. For example, `(preference, ⟨John, L102⟩, strong, very true)` captures both the strength of John's preference for room L102 and the system's confidence in that observation. - Crisp vs. Fuzzy Classes: Even a crisp class like `MeetingRegistration` may own an attribute with a fuzzy range (`participant importance`). Conversely, `ImportantParticipant` is a fuzzy class whose `ISA` membership degree is implicitly derived from static `status` and dynamic `role`. - Soft Constraints: Requirements like "a meeting location should be as convenient as possible for all important participants" are modeled as soft constraints spanning the `prefer` and `take place` associations (Fig. 8).

Root Causes & Resolution Mechanics (Sections 5.3.1 & 5.3.2): 1. Competing Resources: Mutually exclusive resources (e.g., library books desired long-term by patrons vs. kept available by librarians). Resolved via Prevention (introduce a Book Arbitrator) or Compromise (specialize `Patron` into `Student` [2 weeks] and `Faculty` [as needed], or `Books` into `ReservedBook` and `UnreservedBook`, Fig. 10). 2. Divergent Expectations: Opposite expectations on a common property, behavior, or relationship (e.g., `ParticipantPreferenceKnown` vs. `ParticipantPrivacy`). Resolved via Compromise by specializing `Participant` into `PrivilegedParticipant` and `NonPrivilegedParticipant`. 3. Side Effects: Executing a use case for one goal impairs an unrelated goal (e.g., `Accommodate important meetings` canceling a meeting impairs `MeetingRequestSatisfied`). Resolved via Prevention by designing an alternative use case (Fig. 9: exclude conflicting schedules out of date range so no meeting is canceled), or via Compromise/Competition.

Formal Impairment & Integration Walkthrough (Section 5.3.2.3 & Fig. 12): - Impairment Notation: I_G2(G1) denotes the degree of impairment suffered by G1 when G2 is satisfied. If I_G2(G1) > I_G1(G2), achieving G1 incurs less overall impairment than achieving G2. - Note on Table 2 Typography in Original Paper: Table 2 in the 2001 IST print lists G_MRS in cells where the caption and prose (p. 134) explicitly analyze G_MCS (MaxConvenientSchedule) vs. G_AP (AppropriatePerformance). Both importance P(G) and impairment I must align to justify Competition; when they disagree (e.g., G_MCS is more important yet satisfying it severely impairs G_AP), neither goal can be neglected, triggering Compromise via class specialization (`Meeting` -> `ProfessionalMeeting` / `PrivateMeeting`, Fig. 11). - Fig. 12 illustrates the incremental integration flow: starting from the Stable Kernel, `MaxNumberOfParticipants` (no conflict) integrates directly; `SupportFlexibility` is integrated via Prevention (alternative use case without cancellation); and `AppropriatePerformance` is integrated via Compromise (specializing `Meeting`).

Conclusion, Practical Constraints & Future Agenda (Section 6 & Footnote 2): - Key Philosophical Contribution: Rather than treating goal-oriented RE and object-oriented analysis as separate worlds, Lee, Xue & Kuo fuse them so that goal interactions directly dictate the modular structure of the resulting FOOM specification. - Practical Implementation Note (Footnote 2, p. 124): Transitioning from FOOM analysis specifications down to implementation requires language-level support for fuzzy membership functions, fuzzy rule encapsulation, and fuzzy inference engines. - Two Planned Next Steps: 1. Worth-oriented domain (Rosenschein & Zlotkin 1994) for evaluating dynamic object behaviors (statecharts) analytically without full simulation/execution. 2. Large-scale empirical validation on National Central University's real-world university timetabling problem, which involves complex multi-stakeholder soft constraints and competing room/time resources.
