Paper Context & Contribution: - Bibliographic Record: Hsueh, N.-L., Xuan, Z. H., & Daramsenge, B. (2023). "Design and Implementation of Gamified Learning System for Mutation Testing." International Journal of Information and Education Technology (IJIET), 13(7), 1150–1155. DOI: 10.18178/ijiet.2023.13.7.1916. Manuscript received Dec 20, 2022; revised Jan 28, 2023; accepted Feb 12, 2023; supported by MOST grant 110-2221-E-035-031-MY2. - Core Research Problem: While mutation testing is a well-established fault-based method for evaluating unit test suite adequacy, university curricula emphasize coding over software quality assurance, leaving students undertrained in systematic test design. - Dual Artifacts Built: The paper contributes (1) Code Immunity Boost (CIB), a gamified web-based learning platform modeled on vaccine development and human immunity, and (2) Code Vaccine Incubator (CVI), an automated mutant generator that produces large mutant pools from classic mutation-testing benchmarks to serve as the experimental cornerstone. - Author Roles: Nien-Lin Hsueh directed the research design, Zeng Hung Xuan implemented the CIB/CVI software architecture, and Bilegjargal Daramsenge authored the manuscript.

Pedagogical Rationale for the Immune-System Framing: - Cognitive Bottleneck of Manual Mutation Testing: Without tool automation, learners must manually inspect each mutant, mentally simulate or hand-trace program execution against candidate test inputs, record outputs, and tally the mutation score. As program branching complexity rises, this clerical overhead rapidly eclipses conceptual learning and extinguishes student motivation. - Structural Mapping of the Metaphor: CIB reframes mutation testing as a biological defense cycle. The program under test corresponds to the Human Body, injected faults (mutants) act as weakened Vaccines that stimulate defense training, student-authored test cases serve as Leukocytes that hunt and neutralize mutants, and real production bugs correspond to Pathogens prevented by an upgraded immune system. - Instructional Value: Translating abstract fault-injection terminology into a familiar biological narrative lowers the initial conceptual barrier for computer science novices while preserving the exact mathematical rigor of mutation testing.

Structure of the Paper (Sections I to V): - Section I (Introduction): Establishes the imbalance between programming instruction and software testing education, synthesizes findings from Mauricio et al.'s (2018) systematic mapping of 156 SE gamification studies, and positions CIB/CVI relative to Code Defenders. - Section II (Background and Related Work): Formalizes the iterative mutant-killing cycle, equivalent mutants, and the Mutation Score (ms) metric (DeMillo et al., 1978), followed by a critical comparison with Code Defenders (Rojas & Fraser, 2016; 2017; Fraser et al., 2019). - Section III (Game Design and Tool Implementation): Details the immune-system analogy (Fig. 1), three Octalysis core drives, seven classic benchmark programs (Jia & Harman, 2009), fragility-calibrated four-tier level design (Fig. 2), Python unittest gameplay workflow (Fig. 3, Table I), and the six-module system architecture (Fig. 4). - Section IV (Pilot Experiments): Reports a controlled pilot study (N = 10 IECS novices) comparing CIB against manual handwriting across three benchmarks (Tables II to VI; Figs. 5 and 6) using descriptive statistics and an 11-item Likert survey adapted from Barrio et al. (2015). - Section V (Conclusion): Synthesizes empirical trade-offs between automated and manual learning and outlines integration with Online Judge systems.

Literature Grounding & Research Motivation (Section I): - Curriculum Imbalance: Software engineering students receive extensive training in programming, version control, requirements, and architecture, yet software testing and program quality receive disproportionately little pedagogical support (Refs. [1–4]). Competent programmers frequently lack the skills to systematically verify and strengthen their own code. - Evidence from SE Gamification (Mauricio et al., 2018 [15]): A systematic mapping study of 156 papers demonstrated that gamification yields four documented benefits in SE education: (1) higher course participation, (2) improved professional knowledge, (3) stronger adoption of SE practices, and (4) enhanced teamwork skills. Common mechanics include leaderboards, points, milestones, levels, paths, progress indicators, competition, and rewards. - Domain Underrepresentation: Across the 156 mapped studies, gamification concentrates heavily in software design, software process, and requirements analysis, whereas software testing accounts for a notably small fraction. - Pandemic-Era Design Inspiration: Combining the precedent of Code Defenders [16] with public familiarity around vaccination during the COVID-19 pandemic, the authors designed CIB to make fault-based testing both conceptually intuitive and intrinsically motivating.

Pilot Study Inquiry Framework (Section IV.A): - Guiding Overarching Question: The pilot evaluation is organized around the core empirical question, "How do students learn by using the CIB tool?" Rather than claiming definitive statistical proof from a small sample, the study validates operational feasibility, tool usability, and behavioral learning patterns prior to full-scale classroom deployment. - RQ1 (Test Case Productivity & Validity): Operationalized by comparing total submitted test cases and valid (compilable, executable) test cases between the CIB experimental group and the manual handwriting control group across three progressively complex benchmarks (Euclidean GCD, Interpolation Search, Triangle). - RQ2 (Feedback Loop & Invalid Submissions): Examines the proportion of uncompilable or failing submissions across groups and tasks. This directly investigates whether rapid automated error messages encourage iterative trial-and-error refinement rather than discouraging students. - RQ3 (Affective & Cognitive Perception): Assessed via an 11-item, 5-point Likert questionnaire adapted from Barrio et al. (2015) [21], decomposed into Motivation (Q1-1 to Q1-5*), Attention (Q2-1 to Q2-3), and Learning Outcome/Performance (Q3-1 to Q3-3*), where asterisked items specifically measure CIB relative to traditional instruction.

Formal Mechanics of Mutation Testing (Section II): - Theoretical Foundation: Originally introduced by DeMillo, Lipton, and Sayward (1978) [17], mutation testing evaluates the fault-detection adequacy of a unit test suite by generating syntactically similar program variants (mutants), each injecting a small fault into the original program. - Execution & Classification Protocol: The test suite executes against both the original program and every generated mutant. If the outputs match, the test suite failed to expose the injected fault and the mutant remains alive, signaling a coverage gap that requires stronger test cases. If the outputs diverge, the mutant is killed and removed from the active mutant set. - Equivalent Mutants (equiv): Some syntactic mutations preserve the exact semantic behavior of the original program for all possible inputs. Because no test case can distinguish an equivalent mutant from the original program, equivalent mutants are excluded from the denominator of the Mutation Score. - Formula Definition: ms = killed / (all - equiv), bounded in [0.0, 1.0]. The Mutation Score quantifies the proportion of killable mutants detected by the test suite; values closer to 1.0 denote stronger fault-detection capability and more thorough boundary/logic testing.

Comparative Analysis with Code Defenders (Section II.A): - Pioneering Precedent: Rojas and Fraser (2016, 2017) and Fraser et al. (2019) [16, 18, 19] developed Code Defenders, a competitive two-player mutation testing game in which an Attacker crafts subtle mutants to survive while a Defender authors unit tests to kill them. - Two-Player Classroom Limitation: Because Code Defenders originally required at least two human players per match, wide proficiency gaps between paired students frequently cause the weaker participant to experience frustration and disengagement. - Single-Player AI Limitation: Although later versions of Code Defenders added a computer-controlled opponent for solo play, the paper notes that its single-player mode lacks an explicit, quantitative difficulty index and cannot fully replicate the pedagogical balance of well-matched two-player play. - CIB + CVI Design Contribution: To resolve both peer mismatch and uncalibrated solo difficulty, CIB operates as a structured solo progression environment backed by CVI (Code Vaccine Incubator). By indexing pre-generated mutant pools via quantitative mutant fragility across four tiers, learners progress at their own pace from simple to subtle faults while still experiencing both test-authoring and mutant-authoring roles.

Biological Analogy & Metric Translation (Section III.A & III.E): - Dual-Role Conceptual Model (Fig. 1): Students interact with the program-test-mutant triad from two complementary perspectives. Playing the Vaccine role means authoring mutants that challenge existing test suites to expose weak assertions; playing the Leukocyte role means writing unit tests that detect and eliminate mutants, thereby fortifying the Human Body (program) against real Pathogens (software errors). - Immunity Metric: Immunity = Mutation Score (ms) = killed / (all - equiv). In the narrative, higher immunity represents a stronger leukocyte population capable of neutralizing more invading mutants, directly teaching students how test-suite fault detection is quantified. - Vaccine Efficacy Metric: Vaccine Efficacy = 1 - fragility. Mutant fragility measures how easily a mutant is killed by a reference set of test cases (higher fragility means easily detected/trivial faults; lower fragility means subtle, hard-to-kill faults). Consequently, a mutant with low fragility yields high Vaccine Efficacy (closer to 1.0), representing a strong "vaccine" that forces test suites to become more rigorous. - Pedagogical Coupling: Regardless of which role a student plays, every action requires reasoning about the behavioral boundary between the original program and its mutants.

Octalysis Gamification Framework & CVI Benchmark Pool (Section III.B & III.C): - Core Drive 1 (Epic Meaning & Calling): Frames the learning environment as a global effort to combat raging pathogens (inspired by the COVID-19 pandemic). Students believe they are contributing to a mission larger than routine homework by developing vaccines (mutants) and training leukocytes (test cases) to reduce program error risk. - Core Drive 2 (Development & Accomplishment): Operationalizes measurable mastery through status points (Immunity and Vaccine Efficacy) and visual progress bars. Progress bars exploit the psychological urge to fill incomplete graphics, rewarding incremental gains and reinforcing the formal definitions of ms and fragility simultaneously. - Core Drive 3 (Empowerment of Creativity & Feedback): Grants learners agency across both Leukocyte and Vaccine roles. Because unit tests and mutants admit many valid formulations, students combine software engineering knowledge with play experience to iterate creatively on their strategies. - Benchmark Programs & CVI [20]: Following standard mutation-testing literature (Jia & Harman, 2009), the authors selected seven classic algorithmic benchmarks (Triangle, Computing the Median, Euclidean GCD, Fibonacci Search, Binary Search, Interpolation Search, and Leap Year) and built the Code Vaccine Incubator (CVI) tool to automatically generate large mutant pools for CIB levels.

Fragility-Calibrated Level Progression (Section III.D): - Quantitative Difficulty Indexing: Rather than ordering exercises subjectively, CIB uses mutant fragility as the objective standard for calibrating four sequential difficulty tiers: Easy, Medium, Hard, and Difficult. Higher-tier levels contain lower-fragility (harder-to-kill) mutants that demand sharper boundary-value and branch-condition reasoning. - Interface Anatomy (Fig. 2): Each level card presents three structured cues: (1) completion status, displaying an earned rank badge (e.g., S rank) for cleared stages or a lock icon for uncleared stages; (2) level metadata including level index, benchmark program name, and difficulty tier; and (3) dual mastery thresholds. - Dual-Threshold Scaffolding: Every level enforces a Basic Condition (minimum mutation score, such as ms >= 50%) required to pass and unlock the subsequent level, paired with a Better Condition (stretch target, such as ms >= 70% to 80%) that awards bonus points and encourages students to pursue thorough test coverage rather than stopping at minimal compliance.

Python unittest Gameplay & Worked Example (Section III.E, Fig. 3, Table I): - Technical Implementation: CIB is built on Python and its standard unittest framework. In the Leukocyte interface (Fig. 3), students view three coordinated panes: (1) the original Python program under test, (2) the set of active mutants for the level, and (3) an interactive code editor for composing unittest test cases. - Worked Walkthrough (Table I): Consider a level containing 10 mutants (m1 to m10) with a Basic pass threshold of Immunity >= 0.5 (killing at least 5 mutants) and a Better high-points threshold of Immunity >= 0.8 (killing at least 8 mutants). - Round-by-Round Progression: In Round 1, test case t1 kills only m1 and m2 (Immunity = 0.2, Not Pass). Using immediate kill-status feedback, the student expands assertions in Round 2 (t2) to kill m1 and m3 through m6 (Immunity = 0.5, Pass). In Round 3, refined test suite t3 kills m1 through m8 (Immunity = 0.8, Pass with high points). - Cognitive Shift: Eliminating manual dry-running lets students allocate their working memory to analyzing logical divergences between the original code and surviving mutants.

Six-Module Software Architecture of CIB (Section III.F, Fig. 4): - Code Editor: Browser-based front-end editing environment where students author either Python unittest test cases (Leukocyte role) or custom program mutants (Vaccine role), while viewing level instructions and feedback. - Code Runner: Server-side compilation and execution engine that validates and runs user-submitted test cases or mutants. When syntax errors, import issues, or runtime exceptions occur, Code Runner returns immediate Error Messages directly to the user; valid executions forward results to the Metrics Computer. - Metrics Computer: Core mutation-testing evaluation engine that executes test suites against the level's mutant pool (or executes reference tests against a student-written mutant) to calculate Mutation Score (for test cases) and Fragility (for mutants). - Game Manager: Orchestration layer that retrieves level specifications for the Code Editor UI, translates raw testing metrics into Immunity / Vaccine Efficacy game scores, evaluates Basic and Better pass conditions, and updates level unlock states. - Persistent Storage Separation: Code Database stores and manages user-authored test cases and mutants, while Game Database persists level configurations, CVI-generated mutant pools, rankings, and player progression records.

Pilot Experiment Methodology & Controls (Section IV.A & IV.B): - Purpose of Pilot Design: Conducted prior to full-scale classroom deployment to evaluate operational feasibility, implementation cost, tool stability, and early impacts on student test-writing behavior and motivation. - Participant Profile: N = 10 undergraduate students (3 female, 7 male) from the Department of Information Engineering and Computer Science (IECS) at Feng Chia University. Crucially, none of the 10 participants had any prior formal exposure to mutation testing, controlling for baseline domain knowledge. - Experimental vs. Control Protocol: Participants were divided into an Experimental Group using CIB (automated execution, instant error/score feedback, gamified levels) and a Control Group using manual handwriting (designing test cases on paper, manually simulating execution against each mutant, recording outputs, and hand-calculating ms). - Evaluated Benchmarks & Survey: Both groups tackled three benchmarks of escalating control-flow complexity: Euclidean GCD, Interpolation Search, and Triangle. Post-task perception was measured via an 11-item, 5-point Likert questionnaire adapted from Barrio et al. (2015) [21]. Because of the small pilot sample (N = 10), the authors appropriately apply descriptive statistics, boxplot distributions, and qualitative classroom observation rather than underpowered inferential tests.

Empirical Analysis of Submission Volume & Complexity Scaling (Section IV.B, Table II, Fig. 5): - Overall Productivity Gap: Across all assignments, the Control (handwriting) group submitted 18 test cases total (mean 3.6 per assignment, 18 / 5), whereas the Experimental (CIB) group submitted 152 test cases total (mean 30.4 per assignment, 152 / 5), an 8.4-fold increase. - Valid Test Case Output: Filtering out uncompilable or non-executable attempts, the Experimental group still produced 25 valid test cases compared to only 8 in the Control group (3.1x higher valid output). - Interaction with Algorithmic Complexity: Disaggregating Table II by benchmark reveals a sharp divergence as control-flow complexity grows. On Euclidean GCD (compact loop logic), submissions were nearly comparable (13 vs. 11). On Interpolation Search (array indexing and arithmetic bounds), CIB led 34 vs. 4. On Triangle (multi-branch conditional classification), CIB surged to 105 submissions while handwriting collapsed to just 3. - Mechanistic Explanation: Manual mutation testing requires executing the entire pipeline by hand for every candidate test; once branching and mutant count increase, manual execution time scales prohibitively, whereas CIB decouples test design from execution overhead.

Interpretation of Invalid Submissions & Rapid Feedback Loops (Section IV.B, Fig. 6): - Defining Invalid Submissions: A submitted test case is classified as invalid if it fails to compile or fails to execute properly (e.g., Python syntax errors or unittest runtime errors). - The Apparent Paradox: Although the Experimental group constructed substantially more valid test cases (25 vs. 8), its proportion of invalid submissions was also higher than that of the Control group (Fig. 6). - Instant Feedback as Trial-and-Error Catalyst: The authors attribute this pattern directly to CIB's immediate feedback from the Code Runner module. When an error message appears, students rapidly modify and resubmit their code to test hypotheses, even when intermediate patches remain syntactically or logically imperfect. Rather than signaling poorer comprehension, this rapid iteration reflects an active, low-friction trial-and-error learning loop essential to mastering unit test authoring. - Control Group Collapse on Harder Tasks: On Interpolation Search and Triangle, the Control group's valid and invalid submissions both dropped close to zero. Without automated feedback, rising problem difficulty halted student attempts altogether instead of triggering iterative debugging.

Survey Analysis of Motivation (Q1) and Attention (Q2) (Section IV.C, Tables III to V): - Questionnaire Structure: Adapted from Barrio et al. (2015) [21] on a 5-point Likert scale. Shared items enable direct Control vs. Experimental comparison; asterisked items (Q1-4*, Q1-5*, Q3-3*) were administered only to the Experimental group to evaluate gamified design relative to traditional teaching. - Motivation Gains (Table IV): CIB outperformed handwriting on every shared Motivation item: liking the learning method (Q1-1: 4.50 vs. 3.50), awareness of mutation testing (Q1-2: 4.83 vs. 3.50), and desire to explore mutation testing deeper (Q1-3: 4.17 vs. 3.00). The low 3.00 Control score on Q1-3 indicates that manual labor eroded intrinsic interest. Experimental students strongly endorsed level challenge (Q1-4* = 4.33) and preferred CIB over traditional instruction (Q1-5* = 4.67). - Nuance in Attention (Table V in Paper): In the published paper's Table V, the Experimental group scored higher on concentration/few distractions (Q2-1: 4.50 vs. 3.50) and active participation (Q2-3: 4.83 vs. 4.50), while Q2-2 ("high-intensity cognitive thinking") was explicitly highlighted as a special case where the Control group scored higher (4.50 vs. 4.17 in the paper's Table V). - Pedagogical Interpretation of Q2-2 vs. Q2-1: Manual output comparison forced deep, effortful tracing (high Q2-2), creating a vivid mental impression of how mutants behave; however, that same computational burden severely degraded concentration (Q2-1 = 3.50) and long-term motivation (Q1-3 = 3.00).

Learning Outcome (Q3) & Qualitative Classroom Contrast (Section IV.C, Table VI): - Conceptual Understanding vs. Applied Capability: On Q3-1 ("I can understand the approach of mutation testing"), both groups reported very high comprehension (Control 4.75 vs. Experimental 4.67). This confirms that manual paper tracing still builds solid conceptual memory of what mutation testing is. However, on Q3-2 ("I can apply the technique of mutation testing"), the Experimental group reported stronger applied confidence (4.33 vs. 4.00) because CIB provided hands-on execution practice with Python unittest. - Perceived Instructional Advantage: Experimental participants rated Q3-3* ("better learning effect than traditional teaching method") at 4.83 out of 5.00, the highest-scoring comparative item in the entire survey. - Divergent Attitudes Toward Mutant Volume: Qualitative post-experiment feedback revealed a striking contrast. Control group students felt heavily pressured by handwriting effort and explicitly requested reducing the number of mutants to make tracing manageable. Conversely, Experimental group students never viewed the mutant pool as excessive; instead, they expressed heightened enthusiasm for CIB and asked to challenge even more difficult levels. - Synthesis of Averages: Across shared items, CIB led in Motivation (4.50 vs. 3.33), Attention (overall average higher), and applied Learning Outcome, demonstrating that automation preserves conceptual rigor while unlocking practical fluency.

Synthesis of Contributions, Limitations & Research Roadmap (Section V): - Core Empirical Conclusion: CIB successfully demonstrates that gamification paired with automated mutant execution (CVI) resolves the motivational collapse inherent in manual mutation testing education. While manual tracing leaves a conceptual impression, its excessive mechanical difficulty and lack of engagement cause novices to abandon deeper study. - Methodological Caveats of the Pilot: Because this work is a pilot study (N = 10, single department, descriptive statistics only), statistical generalization requires larger controlled classroom cohorts with formal hypothesis testing and longitudinal skill-retention assessments. Additionally, the pilot primarily evaluated the Leukocyte (test-writing) workflow; systematic evaluation of the Vaccine (mutant-writing) role remains an open avenue. - Future Integration Roadmap: The authors highlight three strategic extensions: (1) integrating CIB and CVI into widely adopted Online Judge (OJ) platforms so students routinely evaluate test quality alongside algorithmic problem solving; (2) conducting full-class controlled experiments with inferential statistics; and (3) extending CVI beyond Python unittest to mainstream curricula such as Java and C++.
