Key Takeaway: We use the RACE framework because its Expectation component gives pedagogical guardrails an explicit home as strict output rules. Transition: Let us look closely at each of the three patterns, starting with Guidance.

Key Takeaway: The Guidance Pattern (Guidey) helps students form an initial plan via small-step scaffolding while strictly withholding executable Python code. Talking Points: - Guidey sits at the top-right of the editor for students who understand the problem statement but do not know how to begin. - It permits conceptual hints and pseudo-code so learners can take one concrete step and come back only if still stuck. - On the right, we document real trade-offs: students may feel lower immediate satisfaction, engage in hint-chaining without coding, or face proficiency gaps where pseudo-code feels either too slow or too abstract. Transition: Once students start implementing and hit an error, they transition to our second pattern, Debugging.

Key Takeaway: The Debugging Pattern (Debuggy) diagnoses a concrete failure from the traceback in two structured parts while leaving the actual edit to the student. Talking Points: - Figure 2a shows how Guidey sits at the top-right while Ask Debuggy appears directly beneath the console error output. - Debuggy grounds its response in the exact traceback and failing line, returning a concise Root Cause Analysis followed by a targeted Correction Hint without a full replacement program. - Its main boundary case is Wrong Answer (WA), where there is no traceback, increasing the risk of incomplete diagnosis; beginners may also rely on Debuggy instead of learning to read tracebacks. Transition: Once the code finally passes, our third pattern invites students to reflect on code quality.

Key Takeaway: The Optimization Pattern (Opti) promotes readability and Pythonic idioms after AC without rewriting the student's code, though voluntary adoption proved challenging. Talking Points: - Figure 2b shows Ask Opti inside the submission history tab, designed to deliver a three-part review: a quality summary, one named best practice such as list comprehension, and a non-executable hint. - We transparently report a real deployment finding: our deployed prompt drifted slightly between performance in Role/Action and readability in Context, which we corrected in Table 2. - Even more revealing, because the UI did not lock Opti until AC, many students clicked it while still failing—which we quantify next. Transition: Let us now examine our empirical deployment in a full-semester Python course.

Key Takeaway: Across 315 students and 86 exercises, AI tutors were used in 26.4% of attempts, and two-thirds of AI users combined multiple patterns. Talking Points: - In Fall 2024, students from engineering, business, and social sciences completed 4,982 attempts, of which 1,315 involved AI tutor assistance. - The bar chart shows non-exclusive usage across those 1,315 attempts: Guidey led at 58.8% (773 attempts), Debuggy followed at 47.7% (627), and Opti accounted for 30.6% (402). - Critically, 67.4% of AI-using students combined more than one pattern, confirming that learners value distinct roles rather than a single generic assistant. Transition: Looking deeper at when students invoked each tutor revealed an unexpected mismatch.

Key Takeaway: Debuggy aligned cleanly with testing errors, whereas Opti suffered a striking state mismatch because it was not gated by task state. Transition: Let us synthesize the recurring pedagogical trade-offs we observed across all three patterns.

Key Takeaway: Making AI help more concrete always opens the door to cognitive offloading, so AT-TPL helps educators pinpoint where to place targeted safeguards. Talking Points: - First, on Specificity versus Agency: AT-TPL exposes exact risk points like hint-chaining in Guidey or skipping tracebacks in Debuggy, enabling safeguards such as hint budgets and explain-before-next-hint checks. - Second, on State Gating versus Autonomy: unlocking Opti only after AC eliminates the 23% to 24% error-state misuse, trading off some learner freedom for pedagogical alignment. - Third, effects depend on proficiency: beginners benefit most from Debuggy but risk dependency, while advanced learners find Guidey slow yet gain the most from Opti.
