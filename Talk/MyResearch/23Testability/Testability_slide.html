Bibliographic & Research Overview: This article (Hsueh, Lai, & Tseng, Electronics 2023, 12(22), 4596; published 10 November 2023; funded by NSTC grant MOST112-2813-C-035-075-E) presents the design of Pytutor, a testing-aware Online Judge (OJ) platform, and an empirical investigation of novice learners' programming ability, software-testing ability, and online learning behaviors. Clarification & Scope: - Note on Naming (Section 1): Although Pytutor shares a similar name with the well-known code-visualization website Python Tutor (pythontutor.com), the two systems are completely distinct; Pytutor is a full Online Judge system designed around learner-authored Defining Test Cases (DTCs). - Core Contribution: Combines software engineering metrics (cyclomatic complexity, branch coverage, mutation testing) with cross-platform behavioral telemetry (Pytutor exercise logs + OpenEdu video logs) across a 7-week Python course enrolling both Computer Science (CS) and Non-CS undergraduates.

Empirical Motivation & Central Finding (Table 1): This opening contrast highlights a pervasive behavioral flaw documented in Section 4.5.1 (Table 1) of the paper: - Blind Submission Rate (23%): Even under a punitive Cautionary Principle that deducts 3 points per formal submission, learners formally submitted 23% of exercise attempts without executing a single Open Test Case (OTC) or Defining Test Case (DTC) beforehand. - Under-Utilization of Self-Testing: Learners executed at least one OTC before submission in 59% of exercises, and authored/ran at least one custom DTC in only 44% of exercises, resulting in an average of 1.43 formal submissions per problem. - Design Intervention: Conventional OJs evaluate code exclusively via hidden test suites, inadvertently training novices to use the grader as a trial-and-error oracle. Pytutor embeds DTC authoring, branch coverage, and mutation testing directly into the practice loop to cultivate and measure software-testing ability.

Paper Structure & Section Mapping: The 25-page article is organized into six sections: 1. Introduction (Section 1): Contrasts competition-oriented vs. educational OJs (Wasik [5]), reviews evidence linking testing to code reliability [7–11], introduces Pytutor, and defines RQ1–RQ3. 2. Related Work (Section 2): Surveys three threads: importance of software-testing ability [10, 12–15], automated programming assessment platforms [16–24], and test-case quality / testing education systems [13, 25–29]. 3. Methods (Section 3): Details Pytutor's Django MVT architecture (Fig. 1), UI (Figs. 2–3), Docker sandbox security [30–33], learner workflow (Fig. 4), and mathematical definitions of Resilience r, PAI, TQI, TQI+, and TEI (Equations 1–6, Figs. 5–7). 4. Experiment (Section 4): Describes the 7-week asynchronous OpenEdu + Pytutor course (64 videos, 50 exercises, Fig. 8), participant demographics (69 enrolled, 61 active), data sources, and descriptive statistics (Tables 1–2, Fig. 9). 5. Discussion (Section 5): Answers RQ1 (four ability quadrants, Fig. 10), RQ2 (CS vs. Non-CS, formative, and summative Mann-Whitney U tests, Figs. 11–16), RQ3 (Spearman correlations, Figs. 17–18), comparison with Yang et al. [11], and study limitations. 6. Conclusions & Future Work (Section 6): Summarizes pedagogical takeaways and outlines AI virtual tutoring, enhanced web IDE telemetry, and controlled experiments.

Theoretical Grounding & Pedagogical Rationale: - Role of Online Judges: Educational OJs accelerate feedback, reduce grading overhead, improve learning outcomes, and lower dropout rates (Pereira et al. [4]). Wasik [5] distinguishes competition-oriented OJs (leaderboards, limited languages) from educational OJs (broader language support, gamification), while Fu et al. [6] demonstrated real-time learning analytics dashboards (VisOJ) for tracking student error distributions. - Why Testing Ability Must Be Cultivated: Empirical studies consistently show that testing strengthens specification comprehension and code reliability: Lemos et al. [7] demonstrated positive impacts of testing education on code reliability; Buffardi & Edwards [8] found strong correlations between early testing quality/quantity and assignment grades; Spacco et al. [9] linked testing habits to improved coding practices; and Fidge & Hogan [10] framed testing ability as "understanding problems" vs. programming ability as "solving problems," noting that novices' testing skills lag substantially behind coding skills. - Core Gap: Standard educational OJs only verify submitted code against hidden tests, offering neither practice opportunities nor quantitative evaluation for learner-authored test cases.

Operationalization of the Three Research Questions: Conducted inside a real 7-week asynchronous Python course combining OpenEdu (video lectures) and Pytutor (coding exercises), the study investigates three progressive questions: - RQ1 (Baseline SE Abilities & Online Behaviors): Quantifies learners' distribution across Resilience (r), Programming Ability Index (PAI), Testing Efficiency Index (TEI), four percentile-based ability quadrants (HPHT, HPLT, LPHT, LPLT), submission/testing frequencies, and OpenEdu video engagement (completion rate, playback speed). - RQ2 (Group Comparisons Across Major & Assessment Types): Uses non-parametric Mann-Whitney U tests to evaluate whether CS majors (n = 26) differ from Non-CS majors (n = 35) in r, PAI, and TEI, and how these indicators separate median-split high vs. low performers in formative practice (50 exercises) versus summative assessment (40-minute timed exam). - RQ3 (Correlational Structure): Applies Spearman's rank correlation across ability indicators, video-watching behaviors, and both formative and summative outcomes to uncover how study habits and testing practices relate to programming achievement.

Positioning Pytutor Against Prior Educational Platforms & Studies: - Automated Grading & Scaffolding Platforms: Early systems such as YOJ (Sun et al. [16]) and Sakai (Suleman [17]) automated grading and enabled iterative resubmission, leveraging formative feedback (Carless et al. [18], Malmi et al. [19]). Subsequent platforms added timed competition (Codeflex [20]), multi-modality scaffolding including Parsons and Faded Parsons problems (Codespec [21]), visual algorithm animation (Progressive Blockly [22]), and gamification (2TSW [23], Swacha [24], Code Defenders [15]). None evaluate learner-authored test suites via coverage and mutation analysis. - Testing-Oriented Assessment Systems: Ala-Mutka [13], Vujosevic-Janicic et al. [25], and Montoya-Dato et al. [26] emphasized that weak test suites misjudge buggy code as correct. Jiang & Deng [27] built an online Selenium script practice tool, Kasahara et al. [28] gamified code quality, and UnitJudge (Garcia-Magarino et al. [29]) used unit tests to localize errors in longer assignments, but did not measure coverage/mutation efficiency of student-designed test inputs. - Closest Benchmark (Yang et al., 2020 [11]): Evaluated programming vs. testing ability using coverage and mutation scores, but examined experienced CS undergraduates with prior testing instruction across only 3 timed unit-test assignments and without video/behavioral analytics. Pytutor extends this paradigm to 50 self-paced exercises across CS and Non-CS novices integrated with OpenEdu telemetry.

System Architecture, Security & Pedagogical Rules (Section 3.1, Figure 1): - Django MVT Architecture: Built on Django's Model-View-Template framework for modular extensibility. The Template layer provides a browser-based Python editor and question interface; the View-Control layer invokes the automated assessment engine upon execution/submission; and the Software-Testing module computes branch coverage, mutation scores, and SE indicators (r, PAI, TEI). Design follows Ihantola et al.'s [30] guidelines for usability and safety. - Three-Tier Test Case Taxonomy: 1. Open Test Cases (OTCs): 2 instructor-provided public examples per exercise allowing basic verification. 2. Defining Test Cases (DTCs): Custom inputs authored by learners in Testing Mode; learners must determine expected correctness themselves to probe edge cases. 3. Hidden Test Cases (HTCs): 5 predesigned private test cases executed only during Formal Submission to compute scores. - Pedagogical Rules & Docker Isolation: The Completion Principle permits unlimited resubmissions until passing all HTCs, while the Cautionary Principle deducts 3 points per formal submission to deter blind guessing. Addressing server security risks [31, 32], every code run spawns a fresh ephemeral Docker container [33] that terminates and self-deletes within seconds, containing infinite loops or malicious scripts.

Interface Structure & Behavioral Logging (Sections 3.1.2 & 3.2, Figures 2–4): - Three-Pane Answering Page: Comprises (1) the Question-Information area (title, problem description, and public OTCs, Fig. 2), (2) the Answering area (code editor with Testing Mode vs. Formal Submission Mode), and (3) the Result-Display area (Fig. 3). - Testing Mode Pedagogical Features (Figure 3): Displays raw, unmodified Python compiler/runtime error messages so novices learn authentic debugging directly from stack traces rather than sanitized hints. The lower panel allows entering custom DTC inputs and retains a rolling history of the last 3 test runs for side-by-side comparison. - Formal Submission Safety Net & Workflow (Figure 4): Learners iterate between coding, OTC checks, and DTC verification (or may bypass testing and submit directly). Formal Submission requires explicitly checking a "Confirm Submission" box to prevent accidental -3 point deductions. Every testing event (code snapshot, test input, output) and formal submission (code, passed HTC count, elapsed time) is logged to the database.

Mathematical Formulation of Resilience (r) & PAI (Sections 3.3.1–3.3.2, Equations 1–3): Pure exercise scores fail to distinguish a student who solves a problem cleanly from one who succeeds only after many failed attempts. Extending Xu et al.'s [34] attempt-based proficiency model and Pereira et al.'s [36] resilience construct with McCabe cyclomatic complexity [35], the authors define three linked equations: - Equation (1) Resilience: r = exp(k * (tr - ch_p)), with tuning constant k = 0.1 (chosen via Fig. 5 to provide smooth, non-extreme weighting). Here tr is total setback attempts (syntax errors in testing/submissions plus formal submissions failing to pass all HTCs), and ch_p is the program's challenge level (decision count / cyclomatic complexity). At tr = ch_p (the "resilience origin"), r = 1. Values r > 1 indicate high persistence through setbacks; r < 1 indicates solving with fewer setbacks than expected. - Equation (3) Base-Adjusted Score: s = base + (100 - base) * p, where base is the minimum score awarded for passing at least one HTC and p is the fraction of HTCs passed. - Equation (2) Programming Ability Index: PAI = s / r. At r = 1 ("programming ability origin"), PAI = s. When r < 1 (high efficiency), PAI exceeds s (course mean PAI = 121.80 > 100); when r > 1 (many setbacks), PAI drops below s (Fig. 6). Thus r acts as a dual indicator: persistence on one hand, and inverse coding fluency on the other. Note: unlike Yang et al. [11], code maintainability was omitted because introductory exercises are small.

Mathematical Formulation of TQI, TQI+ & TEI (Section 3.3.3, Equations 4–6): Following software-testing literature linking coverage and mutation killing to fault detection [11, 37], testing ability is quantified exclusively from how much a learner's custom DTCs strengthen the baseline OTC suite: - Equation (4) Test Quality Index: TQI_t = sqrt(b_t * ms_t), the geometric mean of branch coverage b_t (fraction of code branches executed) and mutation score ms_t (fraction of generated mutants killed), scaled 0–100. - Equation (5) Quality Gain Ratio: TQI+ = TQI_OD / TQI_O, comparing the combined OTC + DTC test suite (TQI_OD) against OTC alone (TQI_O). - Equation (6) Testing Efficiency Index: If a learner runs only OTCs (te = te_o), TEI = 1 by default (assuming basic testing competence). If the learner authors DTCs (te > te_o), TEI = TQI+ * (1 + exp(-k * (te - ch_t))), where te = total test cases (OTC + DTC), ch_t = expected test count scaling with complexity, and k = 0.1 (Fig. 7). - Design Safeguard: Adding "1 +" to the exponential term guarantees that learners willing to write DTCs are never penalized below the baseline TEI = 1 even if their DTCs are numerous or weak. Achieving high TQI+ with concise test sets (te < ch_t, left of the "testing ability origin") yields the highest TEI. Course mean TEI was only 1.89 because many exercises remained at TEI = 1.

Experimental Context, Curriculum & Data Sources (Sections 4.1–4.4, Figure 8): - Course Structure: A 7-week asynchronous online Python course combining OpenEdu (7 units, 64 prerecorded videos: Units 1–5 cover programming fundamentals, basic structures/operations, logic, and OOP; Units 6–7 cover practical data processing and object design) with Pytutor (50 exercises distributed across Units 1–5 as 1, 9, 13, 18, and 9 problems respectively). Five face-to-face Q&A meetings (following Montoya-Dato et al. [26] on instructor-guided reflection) and a Microsoft Teams channel provided synchronous/asynchronous support. - Problem Bank Metrics (Figure 8): Mean cyclomatic complexity = 4.06; each problem includes 2 OTCs and 5 HTCs. Baseline OTCs average 0.92 branch coverage and 0.75 mutation score, though Fig. 8 shows several exercises with very low baseline TQI where learner DTCs are essential to catch faults. - Cohort & Filtering: 69 undergraduates enrolled (27 CS, 42 Non-CS; 54 had prior coding experience in some language, 15 had prior Python). Motivations included interest in programming (44), acquiring a second skill (30), online convenience (21), and enrolling with classmates (13). After excluding 8 learners inactive in later weeks, N = 61 active learners (26 CS, 35 Non-CS) entered statistical analysis. - Triangulated Measures: Pre-course survey, OpenEdu video event logs (load_video, play_video, seek_video, pause_video, stop_video), Pytutor execution logs, formative score (50-exercise average), and summative score (40-min, 4-question coding exam held 2 weeks after Week 7 release).

Empirical Analysis of RQ1: Engagement & Ability Quadrants (Sections 4.5 & 5.1, Tables 1–2, Figs. 9–10): - Engagement Telemetry (Table 1): Despite the -3 point cautionary penalty, learners averaged 1.43 formal submissions per exercise (spending 14.45 min/problem on average), with tr = 3.23 setbacks and te = 3.41 total test cases (executing OTCs 3.96 times and DTCs 2.94 times per question, including reruns). Crucially, 59% ran >= 1 OTC, only 44% ran >= 1 DTC, and 23% submitted blindly with zero testing. On OpenEdu, mean video completion was 56% (median 60%, max 83%) at an average playback speed of 2.08x (median 2.16x, max 2.92x), signaling low learner patience. - Proportional Exam Prediction: Given 14.45 min per practice problem, a 40-min, 4-question final exam proportionally predicts ~69% completion; the observed summative mean of 68.04 (median 77.93) closely matches this estimate. - Descriptive Distributions (Table 2, Fig. 9): r (mean 1.97, SD 2.82, median 0.99, max 17.53 — strongly right-skewed due to highly persistent outliers); TEI (mean 1.89, SD 0.49, median 1.92, range 1.00–2.99); PAI (mean 121.80, SD 23.64, median 127.55, range 49.18–157.19); Formative (mean 72.66, median 84.08); Summative (mean 68.04, median 77.93). - Four Percentile Quadrants (Figure 10): Splitting PAI and TEI at percentile rank 50 (1–50 Low, 51–99 High) yields HPHT = 11, HPLT = 20, LPHT = 19, LPLT = 11. Nearly two-thirds (39/61) exhibit mismatched coding vs. testing profiles.

RQ2a Statistical Breakdown: Departmental Background (Section 5.2, Figures 11–12): Because indicator distributions violated normality (skewed r and PAI), two-sided Mann-Whitney U tests were used to compare CS (n = 26) and Non-CS (n = 35) learners: - Programming Ability (PAI, Fig. 12b): Highly significant advantage for CS majors (p = 0.0003 < 0.05). In Figure 11, 19 of 26 CS students (73%) fall into High PAI (HP), compared with only 12 of 35 Non-CS students (34%), and CS distributions are tighter/more concentrated. - Resilience (r, Fig. 12a): Significantly higher among Non-CS majors (p = 0.001 < 0.05). Facing less prior coding fluency, Non-CS students encountered more syntax/logic setbacks yet persisted through repeated iterations. - Testing Efficiency (TEI, Fig. 12c): No statistically significant difference (p = 0.277 > 0.05). Counterintuitively, a higher proportion of Non-CS learners achieved High TEI (19/35 = 54%) than CS majors (11/26 = 42%). - Behavioral Interpretation: The authors infer that prior coding experience gives CS majors confidence to attain high scores without authoring many DTCs, whereas Non-CS learners proactively write custom DTCs to avoid compilation and grading errors. Consequently, CS enrollment alone does not confer software-testing proficiency.

RQ2b Statistical Breakdown: Formative Learning Phase (Section 5.2, Figures 13–14): Splitting the 61 active learners at the formative-assessment median (84.08 across 50 Pytutor exercises) produced 31 high-scoring and 30 low-scoring learners: - Ability Quadrant Distribution (Figure 13): Among the 31 formative high scorers, 21 (68%) belong to the High Testing Ability (HT) quadrants — specifically 9 HPHT and 12 LPHT learners. Notably, 12 learners with below-median programming fluency (Low PAI) still reached the top half of formative scores by leveraging disciplined DTC testing. - Mann-Whitney U Results (Figure 14): - Resilience r (Fig. 14a): p = 0.153 (not significant) — persistence alone does not guarantee high formative scores without effective coding or testing strategies. - PAI (Fig. 14b): p = 0.035 (significant) — higher coding efficiency separates top formative performers. - TEI (Fig. 14c): p = 0.036 (significant) — higher testing efficiency independently and significantly distinguishes high scorers from low scorers during self-paced practice. - Pedagogical Insight: In untimed formative learning, strong testing ability acts as a compensatory scaffold that enables novices to catch edge-case defects before incurring formal submission penalties.

RQ2c Statistical Breakdown: Timed Summative Exam (Section 5.2, Figures 15–16): Splitting the 61 learners at the summative final-exam median (77.93 on a 40-minute, 4-question programming exam held two weeks after Week 7) yields 31 high achievers and 30 low achievers: - Ability Quadrant Shift (Figure 15): In the high-score group, High PAI learners dominate (18 HP vs. 13 LP), reversing the low-score group (13 HP vs. 17 LP). - Mann-Whitney U Results (Figure 16): - Resilience r (Fig. 16a): p = 0.105 (not significant). - PAI (Fig. 16b): p = 0.016 (statistically significant) — programming proficiency is the sole significant differentiator of exam performance. - TEI (Fig. 16c): p = 0.93 (completely non-significant). - Contextual Explanation: Under severe time constraints (~10 minutes per coding problem), learners lack the temporal margin to design comprehensive DTC suites and iterate through coverage/mutation checks. Furthermore, the timed exam emphasizes conceptual mastery and rapid, low-error implementation, giving high-PAI coders a decisive competitive advantage.

RQ3 Correlational Network: Abilities, Video Telemetry & Grades (Section 5.3, Figures 17–18): Spearman's rank correlation heatmaps uncover three structural relationships across the 61 learners: 1. Inter-Indicator Structure (Figure 17): - PAI vs. r = -0.863 (strong negative): Mathematically expected since PAI = s / r, reflecting that fluent coders encounter fewer setbacks. - r vs. TEI = +0.50 (moderate positive): Learners who hit more syntax/logic errors actively engage in multiple rounds of DTC testing to debug their code. - PAI vs. TEI = -0.385 (moderate negative): Confirms that programming fluency and testing diligence are distinct constructs; strong coders often pass introductory exercises without thorough DTC authoring. 2. OpenEdu Video Behaviors vs. Abilities (Figure 18): - Video Completion vs. TEI = +0.331 (moderate positive): Diligent learners who watch more lecture content also test more thoroughly; completion has near-zero correlation with r (+0.111) and PAI. - Playback Speed vs. PAI = -0.24 (negative), vs. r = +0.203 (positive), vs. TEI = +0.237 (low positive): Rushing videos at high speed impairs conceptual comprehension, raising coding errors (lower PAI, higher r) and forcing compensatory testing (higher TEI). 3. Outcomes: PAI correlates moderately with both Formative (+0.35) and Summative (+0.38) scores; TEI correlates positively with Formative (+0.26) but not Summative (+0.073); r shows weak negative correlations with both assessments.

Cross-Study Synthesis & Methodological Limitations (Sections 5.4 & 5.5): - Positioning Against Automated Assessment Platforms: Unlike YOJ [16], Sakai [17], and Codeflex [20], Pytutor lets instructors vary difficulty while requiring learners to design DTCs; like Codespec's [21] multi-skill focus, Pytutor allows instructors to seed starter/buggy code to target debugging skills directly. - Detailed Comparison with Yang et al. (2020) [11]: Both studies converge on a crucial empirical truth — strong programming skill does not guarantee strong testing ability (nearly half of high-PAI coders exhibit low TEI, while low-PAI learners can excel in TEI). Key extensions: Yang et al. studied experienced CS undergraduates with prior testing training on 3 timed unit-test tasks (Pearson r); Pytutor scales to 50 self-paced exercises across CS and Non-CS novices without prior testing training, adds OpenEdu video telemetry, and applies non-parametric Spearman/Mann-Whitney statistics. - Three Explicit Study Limitations (Section 5.5): 1. Ceiling Effect on Baseline OTCs: Several exercises had baseline TQI_O > 80, leaving narrow headroom for learner DTCs to raise TQI+ and compressing TEI variance. 2. Untaught Testing Methodology: Because formal testing techniques (boundary value analysis, equivalence partitioning) were not taught, TEI captured novices' "natural sensitivity" to test cases rather than trained testing skill. 3. Off-Platform IDE Blind Spot: Pytutor cannot observe whether students author and test code locally in an external IDE (e.g., VS Code, PyCharm) before pasting a polished solution into the web editor.

Evidence-Based Recommendations for Programming Curricula (Sections 5.1, 5.2, 5.5 & 6): Translating the empirical findings from 61 learners across 50 exercises into actionable instructional design yields three concrete interventions: 1. Embed Software-Testing Instruction for ALL Majors: In the Taiwanese university context studied, CS majors showed no natural TEI advantage over Non-CS peers (p = 0.277), yet strong testing enabled 12 LPHT novices to reach the top half of formative scores. Introductory programming courses (both CS1 and non-major Python) should explicitly teach black-box test design, boundary cases, and equivalence partitioning alongside syntax. 2. Micro-Segment Video Lectures into Knowledge-Point Clips: Because learners averaged only 56% video completion at 2.08x playback speed — and accelerated viewing correlated negatively with PAI (-0.24) and positively with setbacks r (+0.203) — long recordings should be partitioned into concise, single-concept micro-videos to sustain attention and prevent superficial comprehension. 3. Engineer "Test-Hungry" Exercises: To eliminate the TQI_O > 80 ceiling effect (Section 5.5), instructors should deliberately calibrate public OTC coverage to remain modest (e.g., TQI_O < 70), pairing the -3 point Cautionary Principle with immediate DTC coverage/mutation feedback so students must write meaningful DTCs to uncover hidden edge cases.

Summary of Contributions & Future Research Agenda (Section 6): Pytutor demonstrates how an educational Online Judge can move beyond binary HTC pass/fail grading by integrating learner-defined test cases (DTCs), cyclomatic-complexity-adjusted resilience (r) and PAI, and coverage/mutation-based TEI. Across 61 active learners and 50 exercises, the study establishes that (1) novices exhibit impatient online habits (56% video completion at 2.08x speed, 23% blind submits), (2) CS majors surpass Non-CS majors in PAI (p = 0.0003) but not TEI (p = 0.277), and (3) both PAI and TEI drive formative success while PAI alone governs 40-minute timed exams. Three Future Directions in the Era of AI-Assisted Programming (citing Welsh [38]): 1. Socratic AI Virtual Teaching Assistant: Leverage generative AI to guide learners toward discovering edge cases and debugging strategies through questioning — explicitly avoiding the pitfall of handing out direct code solutions. 2. Enhanced Web IDE & Fine-Grained Telemetry: Upgrade Pytutor's in-browser editor so learners prefer coding on-platform rather than in local IDEs, enabling keystroke-level, edit-compile-test behavioral analytics. 3. Experimental vs. Control Group Validation: Conduct randomized or quasi-experimental controlled trials in future offerings to establish the causal learning gains of testing-integrated OJs over conventional OJs.
