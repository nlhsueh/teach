Paper Metadata & Archival Context: - Full Title: A Cloud Service Implementation for Evaluating Design Pattern in Software Evolution - Publication: Journal of Information Science and Engineering (JISE), Vol. 31, No. 3, pp. 1051–1070, 2015 (Received Oct 16, 2013; Revised Feb 26 & May 24, 2014; Accepted Jun 19, 2014; Communicated by Ce-Kuen Shieh; DOI: 10.1688/JISE.2015.31.3.15). - Authors: Der-Hong Ting (Ph.D. student, FCU), Nien-Lin Hsueh (Corresponding author, Associate Professor, FCU), Chao-Tung Yang (Professor, Tunghai University), and Chin-Hung Chang (Associate Professor, Hsiuping University of Science and Technology). - Core Premise: Builds upon the authors' earlier COMPSACW 2011 Pattern Utilization Analyzer (PUA) by migrating single-node, multi-version design pattern utilization evaluation onto an Apache Hadoop (HDFS + MapReduce) Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) cloud architecture to achieve >10x computation speedup on large open-source software (OSS) histories.

Structural Map of the Paper: - Section 1 (Introduction, pp. 1051–1053): Motivates design pattern roles (quality-improver, activity-facilitator, problem-solver, conflict-resolver), exposes the single-node PUA runtime bottleneck (Table 1), and states the three research contributions. - Section 2 (Background & Related Work, pp. 1053–1056): Contrasts prior single-version or survey-based pattern studies (Vokac, Prechelt, Aversano, Tsantalis, Dong, Zhang & Budgen) and summarizes Hadoop HDFS & MapReduce master/slave architecture. - Section 3 (Utilization Evaluation, pp. 1056–1059): Formalizes PAC(dp, D), PAE(dp), utilization predicate delta(dp, Dj+1), and DoU with a Strategy compression running example and 8-version timeline. - Section 4 (Cloud Service Design, pp. 1059–1062): Details the legacy 4-step web PUA, the new Map/Shuffle/Reduce key-value formulation, and the 3-module PUA Cloud Service architecture. - Section 5 (Experiment, pp. 1062–1068): Evaluates 10 Java OSS projects (5 small, 5 large) across 2 to 19 Hadoop nodes, reporting both DoU results and speedup ratios. - Section 6 (Conclusion, p. 1068): Synthesizes the SaaS software quality evaluation cloud paradigm.

Theoretical & Practical Motivation (Section 1, Table 1): - In earlier work (Hsueh et al., SoSyM 2009), design patterns were classified into four perspectives: activity-facilitator, quality-improver, problem-solver, and conflict-resolver. For example, Observer acts simultaneously as a quality-improver (reducing Subject-Observer coupling), activity-facilitator (aiding decomposition), and problem-solver (preventing data inconsistency). - In COMPSACW 2011, the authors introduced "utilization of deployed design patterns"—the degree of conformance between consecutive version changes and the pattern's intended extensibility. - Table 1 illustrates the severe computational bottleneck of single-machine analysis: while tiny JUnit (91–248 KB) completes in 0.7 minutes, execution time grows super-linearly with source code size (FreeMind 15.4 min, JHotDraw 33.3 min, jMemorize 169.3 min). Later in Table 5, Weka (477.37 min) and jGnash (926.66 min) take 8 to 15+ hours on a single node, rendering interactive developer feedback infeasible.

Research Goals & Contributions (Section 1, p. 1053): 1. Engineering Contribution: Designing and implementing a Hadoop-based SaaS assessment service that parallelizes both pattern detection (Map phase) and pairwise evolution conformance checking (Reduce phase), achieving over 10x speedup on large repositories compared to legacy single-node PUA. 2. Empirical Contribution: Benchmarking both pattern utilization (DoU across 9 GoF pattern categories) and distributed scalability (2 to 19 nodes) across 10 long-lived Java OSS projects harvested from SourceForge. 3. Methodological Contribution: Establishing an automated longitudinal quality-monitoring pipeline that shifts design pattern evaluation from static single-snapshot inspection to continuous evolution tracking.

Literature Positioning & Infrastructure Rationale (Section 2, pp. 1053–1056): - Empirical studies on design patterns (Vokac 2004, Prechelt et al. 2001, Ng et al. 2012, Chen & Huang 2009, Aversano et al. 2009, and Zhang & Budgen's 2012 SLR) reveal mixed effects: patterns do not automatically guarantee lower defect density or easier maintenance unless actually exercised during evolution. - Detection tools such as Tsantalis et al.'s similarity-scoring algorithm (Pattern4, TSE 2006) can detect modified patterns from Java bytecode/source, making it the underlying detector chosen for PUA, whereas DP-Miner (Dong et al., 2007) lacks support for pattern deviations. - Because analyzing N versions of an OSS repository is embarrassingly parallel across versions during pattern detection (Map) and naturally grouped by project for consecutive-version comparison (Reduce), Apache Hadoop (HDFS + MapReduce) provides a natural, fault-tolerant, commodity-hardware foundation.

Formal Definitions (Section 3, Equations 1–6, Figs. 4–6): - Eq. (1) Pattern Application Context: PAC(dp, D) = {<element, r>}. For the Strategy compression example (Eq. 2), PAC(Strategy, D) = {<DataCompression, RC>, <CompressionTool, RS>, <RAR, RCS>, <ZIP, RCS>}. - Eq. (3) Pattern Application Evolution: PAE(dp) = {<role, operator>}, where operator is add (+) or delete (-). For Strategy (Eq. 4), PAE(Strategy) = {<RCS, +>, <RCS, ->}, capturing its intent to add or remove concrete algorithms without modifying Context. - Eq. (5) Utilized Pattern Application: delta(dp, Dj+1) <=> exists Dj such that Delta(PAC(dp, Dj), PAC(dp, Dj+1)) conforms to PAE(dp). Adding "7-ZIP" as a new ConcreteStrategy in Dj+1 satisfies this predicate. - Eq. (6) Degree of Utilization: DoU = (# of utilized applications) / (# of pattern evolutions), where # of evolutions = (alive versions - 1). In Fig. 6's 8-version illustration (V1–V8), dp1 achieves 2/7, dp2 achieves 3/7, dp3 (deployed at V3) achieves 1/5, dp4 (removed after 2 evolutions) achieves 1/2, and unutilized dp5 gets 0/5.

MapReduce Formulation (Section 4.1 & 4.2, Equations 7–8, Figs. 7–9): - Legacy Web PUA (Fig. 7) executed four sequential steps on one machine: (1) download OSS versions from SourceForge, (2) run Tsantalis's Pattern4 detector, (3) serialize PAC to XML (Fig. 8: pattern name, role name, code element location), and (4) compare consecutive PACs against PAE to compute DoU. - Because Step 2 (Pattern4 graph-similarity detection) dominates >95% of runtime, the MapReduce design parallelizes Step 2 & 3 across cluster nodes in the Map phase (Eq. 7: list_m<ProjectName, (Vi, PAC)>). - The Shuffle phase exploits Hadoop's built-in grouping and sorting to align versions V1..Vn per project without custom synchronization code. - The Reduce phase (Eq. 8) performs lightweight pairwise XML diffing between adjacent versions Vi and Vi+1 to evaluate PAE conformance and persist longitudinal DoU statistics.

System Architecture of PUA Cloud Service (Section 4.3, Fig. 10): - The refactored PUA Cloud Service retains three logical modules mapped cleanly onto Hadoop: 1. PAC Modeler: Executes inside Map tasks to invoke the Pattern Detection Tool (Pattern4) on HDFS-resident source archives and construct per-version PAC XMLs. 2. PAE Analyzer: Executes inside Reduce tasks to check Delta PAC conformance against PAE rules across consecutive releases. 3. Evolution Manager: Aggregates utilization counts, manages persistent storage, and serves client-facing DoU analytics as a Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) endpoint. - Decoupling storage (HDFS) from compute (MapReduce) allows elastic horizontal scaling as new versions or additional OSS projects are onboarded.

Experimental Context & Benchmark Selection (Section 5.1, Tables 2–3): - Ten diverse, long-lived Java OSS projects were selected from SourceForge spanning GUI frameworks (JHotDraw), unit testing (JUnit), mind mapping (FreeMind), semantic web (Jena), personal finance (jGnash), flashcards (jMemorize), PDF utilities (PDFsam), image editing (Pixelitor), music tablature (TuxGuitar), and machine learning (Weka). - Total benchmark corpus spans 170 version snapshots totaling ~253.5 MB of source code, with individual versions ranging from 70 KB (FreeMind 0.0.2) up to 6,246 KB (Weka 3.7.5). - Splitting at 10,000 KB total size cleanly separates I/O-sensitive small repositories from compute-bound large repositories, enabling controlled analysis of Hadoop's overhead vs. parallelism payoff.

DoU Empirical Findings (Section 5.2, Fig. 11, pp. 1064): - Nine GoF quality-improver pattern categories were evaluated: Factory Method, Prototype, Command, Composite, Decorator, Observer, State/Strategy (grouped due to identical static class structure in Pattern4), Template Method, and Visitor. - Deployment frequency vs. actual utilization diverges significantly: Command, Template Method, and State/Strategy are the most frequently deployed, whereas Visitor is the least deployed. However, Observer and State/Strategy exhibit the highest actual utilization (DoU). - Practical takeaway for software architects: Observer may not be programmers' most frequently deployed pattern, yet when applied it delivers strong evolutionary flexibility; State/Strategy is both popular and genuinely utilized. Conversely, deploying patterns that sit at DoU ≈ 0 introduces structural complexity without evolutionary payoff.

Small OSS System Benchmarks (Section 5.3, Table 4, Figs. 12 & 14): - Performance improvement ratio is defined as T_PUA / T_Cloud(k nodes). For FreeMind (16 versions), legacy PUA takes 15.40 min; 2 nodes cut it to 9.18 min (1.68x), and 19 nodes reduce it to 1.60 min (9.64x). - JHotDraw (8 versions, 8,977 KB) drops from 33.29 min to 4.64 min at 19 nodes (7.18x). Note mild non-monotonicity between 11–17 nodes caused by scheduling variance and heterogeneous CPU nodes (i5 vs. Core 2 Duo). - JUnit (total 3,079 KB across 19 tiny versions) never beats single-node PUA (0.74 min vs. best 0.91 min at 15 nodes, ratio 0.81x) because Hadoop job initialization, HDFS block distribution, and task scheduling latency outweigh sub-minute pattern detection.

Large OSS System Benchmarks (Section 5.3, Table 5, Figs. 13 & 15): - All five large repositories (>10,000 KB) achieve dramatic runtime reductions: - Jena (17 versions, 37.4 MB): 120.00 min -> 10.64 min at 13 nodes (11.28x peak speedup, best overall). - jGnash (26 versions, 75.5 MB): 926.66 min (15.4 h) -> 94.27 min (1.57 h) at 19 nodes (9.83x). - jMemorize (12 versions, 31.6 MB): 169.32 min -> 24.57 min at 19 nodes (6.89x). - Pixelitor (34 versions, 26.0 MB): 47.46 min -> 6.93 min at 17 nodes (6.85x). - Weka (13 versions, 50.6 MB): 477.37 min -> 191.80 min at 13 nodes (2.49x). Why is Weka's speedup capped around ~2.5x? Because Weka has a single massive latest version (6,246 KB)—a straggler Map task whose single-node Pattern4 runtime bounds total job completion time (Amdahl's Law at version-level granularity).

Critical Discussion & Threats to Validity (Section 5.4, pp. 1067–1068): - Pattern Utilization Takeaways: The divergence between deployment count and DoU underscores why static pattern counting is insufficient for quality assessment—measuring whether the pattern's intended extension points (PAE) are actually exercised across versions reveals genuine maintainability value. - Systems Engineering Trade-Offs: 1. Small-file penalty: Hadoop is optimized for large, compute-heavy workloads; small repositories (<5 MB total) suffer from NameNode/JobTracker coordination and disk I/O latency. 2. Skewed version sizes (straggler effect): In Weka, version size grows from 1.5 MB to 6.25 MB; because one Map task processes one full version, the largest version sets a lower bound on makespan regardless of adding more nodes. Sub-version or module-level partitioning is a natural future optimization. 3. Cluster heterogeneity: Mixing Core i5 and Core 2 Duo nodes explains minor runtime fluctuations when node count changes from 11 to 19.

Conclusion & Broader Impact (Section 6, p. 1068): - This paper bridges empirical software engineering and distributed cloud computing by operationalizing formal design pattern evolution metrics (PAC, PAE, DoU) as a scalable Hadoop MapReduce service. - Key verified numbers: 10 Java OSS projects (170 versions), 19-node Hadoop 0.21 cluster, peak DoU = 0.350 (Observer in JHotDraw), small-system peak speedup = 9.64x (FreeMind, 15.40 -> 1.60 min), large-system peak speedup = 11.28x (Jena, 120.00 -> 10.64 min), and jGnash reduced from 15.4 hours to 1.57 hours (9.83x). - Together, the formal evolution model and SaaS cloud architecture lay the groundwork for an elastic "Software Quality Evaluation Cloud" where developers continuously mine and verify architectural design decisions at scale.
