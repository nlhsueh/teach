Bibliographic & Research Overview: This paper (Lin, Hsueh, & Shen, Fundamenta Informaticae 82, 2007, pp. 155–169, IOS Press) proposes a high-capacity, low-complexity reversible data hiding algorithm grounded in the block difference histogram of non-overlapping two-pixel blocks. Core Contributions: 1. Shifts the histogram of absolute two-pixel block differences d_i = |b_{2i} - b_{2i+1}| rather than raw pixel intensities, producing sharp peaks near zero even on smooth or flat-histogram images (e.g., Gradient, where Ni et al.'s pixel-histogram algorithm hides 0 bits). 2. Alternates horizontal and vertical serpentine scans alongside alternating Difference Directions (DD = down / up) across iterations, preventing cumulative pixel drift while handling boundary pixels cleanly via Saturation Blocks (SB) and Fresh Saturation Blocks (FSB). 3. Achieves 1.52 bpp average max payload (1.41 bpp pure), 1.13 bpp average payload / 1.06 bpp pure payload at PSNR >= 30 dB, O(N) linear time complexity, and bit-exact cover image restoration.

Structural Navigation: The paper is organized into four sections: Section 1 motivates reversible data hiding and compares lossless compression, difference expansion, and histogram shifting; Section 2 details Difference Direction (DD), Saturation/Fresh Saturation Blocks (SB/FSB), the 9-step multi-iteration embedding algorithm (2.1), the 8-step reverse extraction algorithm (2.2), and a complete two-iteration 4x4 example; Section 3 evaluates 8 standard 512x512 grayscale images (Baboon to Gradient) against Ni et al. [16] in capacity, PSNR, overhead, and O(N) runtime; Section 4 concludes.

Motivation & Problem Setting (Section 1, Figure 1): Reversible data hiding (also termed lossless watermarking) requires that after extracting the secret payload from the stego-image, the receiver can mathematically reconstruct every single pixel of the original cover image with zero error (MSE = 0, PSNR = infinity). As depicted in Figure 1, a practical reversible scheme must not rely on large external side channels; instead, it embeds its own recovery overhead concurrently inside the payload space, transmitting only a tiny constant header to the receiver.

Comparative Literature Context (Section 1, p. 156): Ni et al. (IEEE TCSVT 2006) [16] is the primary baseline because it shares the elegant O(N) histogram-shifting paradigm without requiring a full-image location map. However, pixel-intensity histograms of natural images spread across 0..255 (limiting peak height to ~2,000–2,700 pixels in 512x512 Lena) and can be completely flat (e.g., a linear Gradient image has ~1,024 pixels at every intensity and zero empty bins, yielding 0 capacity in Ni et al.). In contrast, neighboring pixels in both natural and synthetic images are strongly correlated, concentrating block differences tightly near 0..5—creating peaks 5x to 15x taller and guaranteed empty bins above d = 130.

Formal Definitions (Section 2, Eq. 1): For an N-pixel grayscale image I, non-overlapping neighboring pixel pairs form blocks (b_i, b_j) with absolute difference d_i = |b_i - b_j|. 1. Difference Direction (DD): Increasing d_i by 1 can either increment the larger pixel (DD = up) or decrement the smaller pixel (DD = down). For tie-breaking when b_i = b_j, Eq. (1) deterministically adds 1 to b_j when DD = up or subtracts 1 from b_i when DD = down, guaranteeing unambiguous reversibility. 2. Saturation Block (SB): A block whose larger pixel is already 255 (when DD = up) or smaller pixel is already 0 (when DD = down). SBs cannot be shifted without overflow/underflow and are skipped (embed nothing). 3. Fresh Saturation Block (FSB): A non-SB block that becomes an SB after shifting by 1 (e.g., (1, 12) becomes (0, 12) under DD = down). Because the decoder cannot otherwise distinguish an original SB from an FSB, only FSB indexes are logged in overhead—avoiding a massive full-image location map.

Two-Pass Per-Iteration Safety Design (Section 2.1, Steps 6–7): Notice why embedding within each iteration is split into two distinct scans (Step 6 and Step 7): - In Step 6, every block that could possibly turn into an FSB (all shifted blocks u < d_i < v, plus peak blocks d_i = u sitting one step away from 0 or 255) is processed first. By the end of Step 6, the exact count S and list of indexes INDs of all FSBs created in this iteration are completely known! - In Step 7, the remaining "safe" peak blocks (d_i = u with max <= 253 for DD=up, or min >= 2 for DD=down) can NEVER become FSBs when shifted by 1. Therefore, embedding (S, INDs, u_{k-1}, v_{k-1}, DD_{k-1}) into these safe blocks cannot trigger any new FSBs—making the overhead self-terminating and 100% deterministic to decode first during extraction. - Special note: For an all-black or all-white image, DD must remain fixed to up or down respectively so pixels do not immediately saturate.

Worked Example Walkthrough (Section 2.1 & 2.2, Figure 3): In Figure 3(a), the 4x4 image is indexed in horizontal serpentine order (top-left pixel 0 = 6, pixel 1 = 7, ..., pixel 7 = 3 below pixel 0). Block 0 = (6,7) has d_0 = 1; Block 1 = (8,8) has d_1 = 0; Block 2 = (8,9) has d_2 = 1; Block 3 = (5,3) has d_3 = 2; Block 4 = (6,7) has d_4 = 1; etc. In Iteration 1 (DD = down, u = 1, v = 5), blocks 0, 2, 4 embed bits "0", "1", "1": block 0 stays (6,7), block 2 becomes (7,9) [d=2], block 4 becomes (5,7) [d=2]; meanwhile blocks with 1 < d < 5 decrement their smaller pixel by 1. In Iteration 2 (DD = up, u = 1, v = 3), vertical serpentine pairing yields new block differences where blocks 4, 5, 7 have d = 1 and embed "0", "1", "0", producing Figure 3(c). During reverse extraction, the decoder starts at Iteration 2 (even -> vertical blocks, DD = up, u = 1, v = 3), recovers "010" and restores pixels 3, 7, 10 by subtracting 1, pushes "010" onto the stack, then runs Iteration 1 (odd -> horizontal, DD = down, u = 1, v = 5) to recover "011", add 1 to pixels 4, 7, 8, 11, 12, 14, and pop "011010" with exact original image recovery.

Reverse Extraction Correctness (Section 2.2, Eq. 2, Figure 4): Because embedding Pass 1 (Step 6) runs before Pass 2 (Step 7) in each iteration, extraction reverses the order: Step 3 reads the safe blocks first (which never became FSBs) to immediately recover S_k and INDs_k. Armed with INDs_k, Step 5 unambiguously distinguishes true FSBs (which must be restored via Eq. 2 and yield bit "1" if d_i = u + 1) from pre-existing SBs (which remain untouched). Pushing Step 5's bits onto the LIFO stack followed by Step 3's bits guarantees that popping the stack after all n iterations reconstructs MSG_1 || MSG_2 || ... || MSG_n in the exact original bit order while leaving every pixel bit-for-bit identical to the cover image.

Test Suite & Overhead Analysis (Section 3, Tables 1–2, Figure 5): Figure 5 spans the entire roughness spectrum—from highly textured Baboon (peak ~12,000 blocks) through standard natural images Lena, GoldHill, Boat, Tiffany, Pepper, and Airplane (peaks 20,000–35,000 blocks) to the synthetic linear Gradient image (peak ~131,000 blocks at d = 0/1!). In Tables 1 and 2, when an image has few or zero FSBs (which holds for almost all natural images at PSNR >= 30 dB), the total per-iteration overhead is only 13 bits (first iteration) or 30 bits (remaining iterations)—meaning 23–24 iterations consume only ~700 bits total, less than 0.3% of the payload!

Analysis of Table 3 (Section 3, pp. 165–166): Table 3 pushes each image to its natural termination point (where the next iteration's FSB overhead exceeds its available peak capacity). Across all 8 images, total payload averages 397,989 bits (1.52 bpp) and pure user payload averages 368,520 bits (1.41 bpp). Pepper provides an instructive edge case: despite being visually smooth, Pepper contains many dark/bright pixels near 0 and 255, triggering substantial FSB overhead in early iterations (38,869 bits of overhead over 28 iterations) and stopping at iteration 29 with a high 28.9 dB PSNR. Conversely, Lena incurs only 10,303 bits of overhead across 72 iterations (2.7% overhead ratio), and Tiffany incurs only 6,468 bits across 49 iterations (1.8% overhead ratio).

Analysis of Table 4 (Section 3, pp. 165–166): When visual quality is constrained to PSNR >= 30 dB (standard imperceptibility floor for high-payload reversible hiding), all 7 natural images run for 23–24 iterations (PSNR 30.1–30.2 dB) and Gradient runs all 27 iterations (30.4 dB). Exact Table 4 overhead (Total minus Pure) highlights the efficiency of FSB-only tracking: - Baboon: 135,844 - 134,831 = 1,013 bits - Boat: 242,301 - 241,594 = 707 bits - Lena: 231,971 - 231,264 = 707 bits - Airplane: 289,877 - 289,174 = 703 bits - Tiffany: 262,514 - 261,841 = 673 bits - GoldHill: 234,160 - 233,457 = 703 bits - Pepper: 268,042 - 238,065 = 29,977 bits (due to near-0/255 boundary pixels) - Gradient: 699,813 - 583,845 = 115,968 bits (still delivering 583,845 pure bits = 2.23 bpp!).

Why Multi-Iteration Capacity Scales Better (Section 3, Figure 6): Figure 6 directly visualizes the structural reason behind our algorithm's dramatic advantage over Ni et al. [16]. In Figure 6(a)-(b), Ni et al.'s pixel-value histogram splits existing peaks in half each time a peak embeds random 0/1 bits, rapidly flattening the histogram into jagged spikes below 2,500 pixels. In Figure 6(c), our block-difference histogram not only starts ~8x taller, but alternating horizontal and vertical pixel pairings continually re-exposes fresh low-difference pairs, keeping peak heights above 7,000 blocks even after 10 full passes.

Rate–Distortion & Complexity Verification (Section 3, Figure 7, pp. 166–167): Figure 7 plots the operational PSNR-versus-payload curve across the first 40 iterations on Lena (smooth/moderate) and Baboon (highly textured). At every single PSNR level from 25 dB to 52 dB, our algorithm dominates Ni et al. [16] by a wide margin. Complexity breakdown: Each embedding iteration performs 3 linear scans (Step 2 pairing/histogram, Step 6 shifting/FSB logging, Step 7 overhead+payload embedding), searches 256 histogram bins in O(1), and modifies at most floor(N/2) pixels by 1 -> O(3nN). Each extraction iteration performs 2 linear scans (Step 3 safe blocks, Step 5 boundary/shifted blocks) -> O(2nN). Since n is small and independent of N, both run in strict linear time O(N).

Summary of Conclusions (Section 4, p. 168): The proposed block-difference histogram shifting algorithm simultaneously solves the three classic trade-offs of reversible data hiding: 1. High Capacity + High Visual Quality: Surpasses 1 bpp pure payload at PSNR >= 30 dB across diverse roughness levels (from Baboon to Gradient). 2. Minimal Overhead without Location Maps: Self-chained FSB indexing requires only hundreds of bits for most natural images, transmitting only (u_n, v_n, DD_n, n) externally. 3. Simplicity & Linear Speed: Easy to implement in O(N) time (58 ms per embed+extract iteration on a 2.4 GHz Pentium 4), making it practical for real-world lossless watermarking and steganography.
